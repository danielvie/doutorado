ARTICLE.TEX · SECTION 3 · PROPOSITION 2

One Function That Only Falls

A tutorial on the stability argument behind dwell-time conditioning: what a Lyapunov function is, how this paper builds one, and why throttling the controller by a factor β that changes every cycle cannot break it.

00

What this document assumes, and where it goes

It assumes you can multiply a matrix by a vector and that you have met eigenvalues once. Nothing else. Everything about Lyapunov functions, positive-definite matrices, induced norms and semidefinite programs is built here from the ground up, in the order the paper needs it.

The paper's control loop is short enough to state in one paragraph. A converter repeats a fixed sequence of nine switch configurations every 286 µs. Once per cycle a static feedback law looks at the error and asks for small displacements δτraw = −K ek of the eight interior switching instants. Those displacements can demand an interval shorter than the hardware allows, so a conditioner multiplies the whole requested vector by the largest factor βk ∈ [0,1] that keeps every interval legal, and the plant executes βkδτraw instead.

That last step is what needs defending. The controller was designed on the assumption that its action would be applied in full. It is not. A number chosen by the plant, different on every cycle, scales it down. The three parts below answer, in order: what would a stability proof even look like; what proof does the paper give for the unthrottled loop; and why does the throttle leave that proof standing.

Part one
Lyapunov from zero. One number that always falls; why it has to be shaped like an ellipse; why eigenvalues are not enough; why two stable matrices can be unstable together; and where the ellipse itself comes from.
Part two
The paper's certificate. What the state actually is here, the two matrices Φ and Acl, and the offline semidefinite program that finds one P for both.
Part three
Why β is harmless. Conditioning lands on a straight segment between those two matrices, the segment lies inside a convex set of contractions, and three lines finish it.

Part one

How a Lyapunov argument works

The tool is a hundred and thirty years old and the idea fits in one sentence: if you can find a quantity that is positive away from the target and strictly decreases every step, the system has to reach the target. The work is all in finding the quantity — sections 02 to 06 say what it has to do, and section 07 says where to get one.

01

The question a simulation cannot answer

Take a system that updates once per cycle by a fixed matrix:

ek+1 = A ek,    ek ∈ ℝn is the error at the start of cycle k

We want to know whether ek → 0. Simulating settles one initial condition. There are infinitely many, and later there will be infinitely many systems too, one per possible value of β. So simulation is evidence, never proof.

Three properties are worth naming now, because the paper claims exactly the third.

stable
Small initial errors stay small forever. They need not shrink.
asymptotically
stable
Stable, and the error goes to zero.
exponentially
stable
The error goes to zero at least as fast as c·qk for some q < 1. This is the useful one: it names a rate.

The obvious first move is to look at the eigenvalues of A. If every eigenvalue has magnitude below one — if ρ(A) < 1, called Schur stable — then Ak → 0 and the system is exponentially stable. That is a real theorem and we will use it. It is also, on its own, a trap for two separate reasons, and both of them bite in this paper. Sections 05 and 06 are those two reasons.

02

One number that only falls

Lyapunov's idea replaces the vector ek, which is hard to reason about, with a single scalar summarizing how far from the target we are. Call the scalar V(e) and demand two things of it:

V(e) > 0 for e ≠ 0,  V(0) = 0
it is a genuine measure of "how wrong we are", zero only when we are right
V(ek+1) < V(ek) always
every cycle strictly improves it, from every starting point

If both hold, the conclusion is forced. V is a non-negative sequence that strictly decreases, so it converges; with a mild strengthening — that it decreases by at least a fixed fraction, not merely by something — its limit is zero, and since V vanishes only at the origin, the error goes to zero. No trajectory anywhere can escape, because the argument never referred to a particular trajectory.

The physical picture is energy. A struck bell is not analyzed by tracking every molecule; it is analyzed by noting that its energy is positive and that damping removes some of it every cycle. V is a made-up energy for a system that may have no physical energy at all.

Why a decreasing V is stronger than a decreasing error A trajectory can be heading for the origin while its distance to the origin goes up for a while — think of a skateboarder in a bowl, gaining height on one side while losing energy overall. Distance is the wrong bookkeeping. V is bookkeeping chosen to be monotone, which is what makes the argument airtight. Section 05 shows this happening for real, in this paper's own matrix.
03

Choosing V: quadratic forms and what P does

"Find a function" is not a method. Nearly all of control theory narrows the search to one family, the quadratic forms:

V(e) = eTP e,    P = PT (symmetric)

Written out for two states, with P = [p₁₁ p₁₂ ; p₁₂ p₂₂], this is p₁₁e₁² + 2p₁₂e₁e₂ + p₂₂e₂² — a weighted sum of squares and cross-terms. Symmetry costs nothing, since any non-symmetric part cancels in the product anyway.

The first requirement, V(e) > 0 for every e ≠ 0, is exactly the definition of P being positive definite, written P ≻ 0. Equivalently: every eigenvalue of P is positive. So the first half of the job reduces to a property of a matrix.

The geometry is worth carrying around. With λi the eigenvalues of P and vi its eigenvectors, the level set eTPe = c is an ellipsoid whose axis along vi has half-length √(c/λi). Big weight, short axis: directions P weights heavily are directions the level set refuses to extend in. Choosing P is choosing the shape of the ruler.

Two norms, and the constant relating them A positive definite P defines a distance of its own, ‖e‖P = √(eTPe), the P-norm. It obeys all the usual rules, including the triangle inequality — which is the entire punchline of part three. It is never wildly different from ordinary distance: √λmin·‖e‖₂ ≤ ‖e‖P ≤ √λmax·‖e‖₂. So a bound in the P-norm converts into a bound in ordinary distance, at the cost of the factor √(λmax/λmin). For the P this paper computes, that factor is 21.9 — and section 05 shows the ordinary error really does swell, by a factor of 1.76, on the way down. Well inside what the certificate allows, and invisible to the P-norm.
04

The test: one matrix inequality

Now impose the second requirement on V(e) = eTPe along ek+1 = Aek. The algebra is three lines.

V(ek+1) = (Aek)TP(Aek)substitute the update
= ekT(ATPA)eksince (Ae)T = eTAT
ΔV = ekT(ATPA − P)eksubtract V(ek), factor

Demanding ΔV < 0 for every non-zero e is, word for word, the statement that the symmetric matrix in the middle is negative definite. The second requirement collapses to

ATPA − P ≺ 0

This is the discrete-time Lyapunov inequality, and three things about it matter.

It can be given a rate. Negative definiteness alone gives decrease but not speed. Replace it by the scaled version

ATPA ⪯ q2P   ⟺   V(ek+1) ≤ q2V(ek)   ⟺   ‖ek+1‖P ≤ q‖ek‖P

and the conclusion becomes quantitative: ‖ek‖P ≤ qk‖e0‖P, exponential stability with the rate written down. The smallest q that works has a name — the induced P-norm of A:

‖A‖P = maxe ≠ 0  ‖Ae‖P ⁄ ‖e‖P

Read it as the worst stretch factor A can apply to any vector, measured with the P-ruler. It is computed, not searched for: ‖A‖P is the square root of the largest μ solving det(ATPA − μP) = 0, a generalized eigenvalue problem your linear-algebra library already has. Two of its properties do all the work later. It is a genuine norm on matrices, so it obeys the triangle inequality ‖X + Y‖P ≤ ‖X‖P + ‖Y‖P; and it is submultiplicative, ‖XY‖P ≤ ‖X‖P‖Y‖P.

Such a P always exists for one Schur matrix. If ρ(A) < 1 then Ak → 0 quickly enough that

P = Σk ≥ 0 (AT)kAk   converges, and satisfies   ATPA − P = −I

So for a single fixed matrix, Lyapunov theory tells you nothing the eigenvalues had not already told you. Its value appears the moment there is more than one matrix — which is exactly this paper's situation.

Finding P is a convex problem. Look at what is unknown. A is given, P is the variable, and ATPA − P is linear in P. A constraint of the form "this symmetric matrix, linear in the unknowns, is negative definite" is called a linear matrix inequality. The set of P satisfying one is convex, several can be imposed at once, and reliable solvers either return a solution or prove that none exists. That is the machinery part two uses offline, and it is why asking for one P that works for two different matrices is a reasonable request rather than a wish.

05

Trap one: eigenvalues describe the end, not the trip

If ρ(A) < 1 already guarantees decay, why bother with P? Because it guarantees decay eventually. It says nothing about what happens first, and "first" can last a long time.

Here is this paper's own open-loop cycle matrix, in the normalized coordinates the design uses:

Φ = [  0.99953  −0.00375   0.01123 ;   0.00088   0.99992   0.00364 ;   0.00556  −0.00028   0.75174 ]
eigenvalues 0.99985 ± 0.00190i,  0.75149   ⟹   ρ(Φ) = 0.999850 < 1   Schur: the open loop does converge

And yet ‖Φ‖₂ = 1.001254 > 1. There are error directions that one uncontrolled cycle makes bigger. Iterate, and it compounds.

In the language of section 03: V(e) = eTe, the obvious choice P = I, is not a Lyapunov function for this system. Spheres are the wrong shape for it. The P that part two computes has a different shape, and in that ruler the very same matrix contracts, ‖Φ‖P = 0.999939 < 1. Nothing about the plant changed. The ruler did.

What a P-norm certificate does and does not forbid It does not forbid the ordinary error from growing; it bounds how much. Combining ‖ek‖P ≤ qk‖e0‖P with the conversion constant of section 03 gives ‖ek‖₂ ≤ 21.9·qk‖e0‖₂. The worst-case swell of 1.76 seen above sits well inside that allowance. A certificate is a promise about the destination plus a ceiling on the detour.
06

Trap two: two stable matrices, one unstable system

The second trap is the one that makes this paper's proposition necessary. Suppose the update matrix is not fixed. Every cycle, something outside your control picks one of two matrices:

ek+1 = Mσ(k) ek,   σ(k) ∈ {1, 2}

Both are Schur. Surely the switched system is stable? It is not, and the counterexample is small enough to check by hand.

The technical reason is that the spectral radius is not submultiplicative: ρ(M₁M₂) ≤ ρ(M₁)ρ(M₂) is simply false — here it would read 4.49 ≤ 0.25. Eigenvalues of individual matrices tell you nothing about products of different matrices. Induced norms, however, are submultiplicative. That is the whole reason the next idea works.

The fix: one P for all of them

Suppose a single P ≻ 0 satisfies ‖M₁‖P ≤ q and ‖M₂‖P ≤ q with q < 1. Then for any switching sequence whatsoever,

‖ek‖P = ‖Mσ(k−1) ⋯ Mσ(0) e0‖P ≤ qk‖e0‖P

because each factor shrinks the P-norm by at least q, whichever factor it happens to be. Such a P is a common quadratic Lyapunov function: the same V falls no matter who is choosing.

This is strictly stronger than each matrix being Schur. The counterexample proves it: that pair diverges under alternation, so no common P can exist for it, even though both matrices are Schur. The converse also fails — a common P is sufficient, not necessary, and some switched systems are stable without one. What makes it the right tool here is that it is checkable: by section 04 the search is a pair of linear matrix inequalities in the single unknown P, solved once, offline.

Hold on to this A controller whose action is rescaled by a number the plant chooses afresh each cycle is a switched system, with one matrix per possible value of that number. Part three is about the special structure that makes this particular family safe.
07

Where V actually comes from

Section 04 said what P must satisfy. It did not say where to get one, and that is the question everyone asks first. The honest answer has two halves.

For a general nonlinear system there is no algorithm. Lyapunov's theorem tells you what a certificate looks like, not how to find it, and constructing one is a genuine research problem in each new case. In practice candidates come from three places: physical energy, when the system has any; an accumulated cost, when you can define one; or a parameterized family whose coefficients you fit numerically. The last of those is what turns the art back into a computation.

For a linear system the question is settled. Restricting the search to quadratics costs nothing at all, because of a converse theorem: if ρ(A) < 1 then a quadratic Lyapunov function exists. Searching among ellipsoids can therefore never miss a stable linear system, and section 04 even wrote one down. So for the systems in this paper, "how do we choose V" is really "how do we choose P", and there are exactly three routes to it.

route 1
Pick Q, solve for P. One matrix, one linear solve. This is the workhorse.
route 2
Take the one your controller already produced. LQR hands you a P whether you asked for it or not.
route 3
Make P the unknown of a convex program. The only route that survives when one P has to serve several matrices — which is this paper's case.

Route 1 — choose what you charge for, and P follows

Turn the Lyapunov inequality into an equation. Pick any symmetric Q ≻ 0 and demand that V fall by exactly eTQe each cycle:

ATPA − P = −Q   the discrete Lyapunov equation, unknown P

This is not a search. Written out, it is a system of linear equations in the n(n+1)/2 distinct entries of P — six numbers when n = 3 — with a unique solution whenever no two eigenvalues of A satisfy λiλj = 1, which every Schur matrix comfortably obeys. Any linear-algebra library solves it in microseconds; in MATLAB it is dlyap(A', Q). Section 04 already gave the solution in closed form, P = Σk (AT)kQAk.

That closed form is worth reading rather than skimming, because it says what Q means:

V(e) = eTPe = Σk ≥ 0 (Ake)TQ(Ake) = Σk ≥ 0 ‖ek‖²Q

Q is a price list: how much you charge for error in each direction. V(e) is then the total bill the system will run up, from now until the end of time, if released from e and left alone. Of course it decreases every cycle — one cycle's worth of the bill gets paid. That is the whole content of the equation.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both matter later. Choosing Q = I means "charge for total squared error, equally in all directions" — the default, and the reason dlyap(A', I) is what people type without thinking. And changing Q changes the shape of the resulting ellipsoid, without ever endangering the fact that it is a valid Lyapunov function for A. Every Q ≻ 0 gives a working P for that one matrix. This is the free parameter: the uniqueness above is uniqueness of P given Q, and says nothing about P being the only valid ruler.

Route 2 — LQR already gave you one

The gain K in this paper comes from a discrete LQR design, which minimizes Σk (ekTQnek + vkTRnvk). Solving it produces, as a by-product, the matrix S of the algebraic Riccati equation, and the optimal cost-to-go is eTSe — a quadratic that decreases along the closed loop by construction, for exactly the reason route 1 gives. So the moment you run dlqr you are already holding a Lyapunov function for Acl. Free, and no extra solve. Whether it is the right one is the next subsection.

Route 3 — let a solver choose P

Routes 1 and 2 both start from one matrix and produce a ruler tailored to it. This paper needs a ruler that works for two matrices at once, and there is no formula for that: no way to combine the P of Φ with the P of Acl into something valid for both. What there is instead is section 04's observation that each requirement is linear in P, so you can hand both to a convex solver as constraints and let it find a point satisfying them simultaneously — or prove that no such point exists. That is the semidefinite program in section 10, and route 3 is the only one of the three that can deliver what Proposition 2 needs.

How good can any ruler be?

Since P is a free choice, it is fair to ask how much of this paper's weak-looking bound 0.999939 is the ruler's fault. Almost none of it, and there is a hard floor that settles the matter. For any P whatsoever,

‖A‖P ≥ ρ(A)   an induced norm can never fall below the spectral radius

because an eigenvector is stretched by exactly |λ| in any norm you care to use. Applied to both endpoints at once, no common ruler can ever certify a factor better than

q ≥ max( ρ(Φ), ρ(Acl) ) = max( 0.999850, 0.568151 ) = 0.999850

The paper's 0.999939 sits 9 × 10⁻⁵ above that floor. Bisecting the semidefinite feasibility problem on q pushes the best common quadratic bound down onto the floor itself, 0.999850, to within solver tolerance — and that P is far better conditioned, at 3.93 instead of 478. So a better ruler is available, but the improvement in the bound is in the fifth decimal place. The conservatism is not coming from the choice of V. It comes from insisting on a guarantee that covers β = 0 forever, and at β = 0 the plant really does crawl at ρ(Φ) = 0.999850. No Lyapunov function can rescue you from a slow open loop; the certificate simply reports it.

Worth trying in the paper The bound is nearly floor-optimal either way, but the conditioning of P is not: cond(P) = 478 means the transient allowance of section 05 is 21.9, while the row above with cond(P) = 37 gives 6.1 for the same certified bound, and the bisection ruler with cond(P) = 3.93 gives 2.0. If a statement about how far the error can swell before it settles is ever wanted, the ruler is worth re-picking — the current SDP maximizes the decrease margin, which is a different objective from keeping the ellipsoid round.
A ruler you have already chosen without noticing The design is done in normalized coordinates, z = Sx−1e with Sx = diag(10, 20, 1). In the original coordinates that is the same as starting from P = Sx−2 instead of P = I: a hand-picked diagonal ruler that says a volt of capacitor error and an amp of inductor error should count comparably. Scaling a plant and choosing a quadratic weight are the same act.

When no quadratic works

A common quadratic is sufficient for stability under arbitrary switching, never necessary. If the solver comes back infeasible, the system may still be perfectly stable — you have only learned that no single ellipsoid certifies it. The standard escalations, in rough order of effort:

multiple / switched
Lyapunov functions
one Pi per mode, with matching conditions at the switches — weaker requirement, more constraints
piecewise quadratic
a different ellipsoid in each region of state space, glued together
polyhedral
V(e) = ‖We‖∞ — level sets are polytopes rather than ellipsoids; always exists for a stable switched linear system, but the polytope can need very many faces
sum of squares
polynomial V of degree 4, 6, … found by a larger semidefinite program; the quadratic case is the degree-2 corner of it
joint spectral
radius
compute the true worst-case growth rate of all products directly, and accept that it is expensive

None of that is needed here, which is the quiet good news of the numbers above: the simplest tool in the list already closes the argument for this loop.

The recipe, condensed 1. Write down every matrix the closed loop can actually run — here, the two endpoints, and by convexity everything between them. 2. If there is only one, solve ATPA − P = −Q with Q = I and stop. 3. If there are several, put one P and one inequality per matrix into a semidefinite program. 4. Check ‖Ai‖P < 1 for each, which is the number you will actually quote. 5. If it is infeasible, either the system is not stable under arbitrary switching, or you need a richer V — the list above says which knobs exist.
Part two

How the paper applies it

Three moves. Turn a periodic trajectory into a fixed point so that "stability" means what part one means. Linearize the cycle to get matrices. Then run one semidefinite program, offline, to find a single P that works for both of them.

08

What the state is: one dot per cycle

The plant is a converter, running in continuous time, switching between nine affine dynamics inside every 286 µs cycle:

ẋ(t) = Aσ(t)x(t) + bσ(t)

The target is not a point. It is the reference periodic trajectory: the closed loop in state space that the nominal switching schedule traces out, over and over. Part one has nothing to say about orbits, only about errors going to zero. The bridge is to stop watching continuously and look only once per cycle, always at the same phase — at the cycle start.

Do that and the picture changes completely. One cycle of the plant becomes one map,

xk+1 = 𝒫(xk, δτk)   exact: nine matrix exponentials, in order

and the reference trajectory becomes a single point x★ that this map leaves where it is — a fixed point, called the cycle anchor. The whole loop reduces to the error ek = xk − x★ and the question of part one: does it go to zero?

Two things are fixed by construction and never in question: the order of the nine modes, and the total period. The controller's only actuator is where the eight interior switching instants sit inside the cycle, gathered in the offset vector δτk. Because moving an instant lengthens one interval by exactly what it shortens the next, the period survives any offset at all.

Why this is legitimate Sampling a periodic system once per period is the classical Poincaré construction. Asymptotic stability of the fixed point of the sampled map is equivalent to asymptotic stability of the periodic trajectory it came from, with phase locked because the period here is fixed by fiat rather than free to drift.
09

The two matrices

The map 𝒫 is not linear, so part one does not apply to it directly. Section 2 of the paper linearizes it around the anchor and the nominal schedule, which yields

ek+1 = Φ ek + Γτ δτk + 𝒪(‖·‖²)

Φ is the nominal cycle matrix: what one cycle does to an error when the schedule is left alone. Γτ says what displacing each switching instant does. Everything below is stated in the normalized coordinates the design uses, z = Sx−1e and v = δτ/ts, which put the three states and eight timings on comparable scales; Φ and Acl below are the normalized ones.

The feedback law is static, one matrix multiply, and it defines the second matrix:

δτrawk = −K ek   ⟹   Acl = Φ − ΓτK

So the two matrices in play are the two extremes of the controller's authority: with the correction switched off entirely, and with it applied in full.

10

The semidefinite program that finds P

This is the only optimization anywhere in the method, it runs once on a workstation, and its output is a single 3×3 matrix. Here is the paper's implementation, from scripts/generate_results.m, with the comments added:

P_variable = sdpvar(state_count, state_count, 'symmetric')
decrease   = sdpvar(1)

constraints = [ P_variable >= 1e-8 * eye(state_count) , ...   % P is positive definite
                trace(P_variable) == 1 , ...                  % fix the scale of P
                decrease >= 0 , ...
                Phi' * P_variable * Phi - P_variable <= -decrease * eye(state_count) , ...
                Acl' * P_variable * Acl - P_variable <= -decrease * eye(state_count) ]

optimize(constraints, -decrease, options)                   % maximize the decrease

Line by line, against part one:

P ⪰ 1e−8·I
V(e) = eTPe must be a real measure of error, so P ≻ 0. The tiny margin is there because solvers work with closed sets and "strictly positive" is not one.
trace(P) = 1
If P satisfies every inequality then so does 10P, and so does 1000P — the constraints are homogeneous. Without a normalization the solver would chase that free scale forever. Fixing the trace picks one representative of each family and makes the decrease margin a comparable number.
ΦᵀPΦ − P
⪯ −γI
The Lyapunov inequality of section 04 for the first endpoint, with a uniform margin γ instead of plain negative definiteness.
AclᵀPAcl − P
⪯ −γI
The same inequality for the second endpoint, sharing the same P. This one line is what makes the certificate common rather than two separate certificates.
max γ
Any feasible P would do; maximizing the margin picks the one that satisfies both inequalities with the most room, which keeps the answer away from the numerical edge.

Every constraint is linear in the unknowns (P, γ), so this is a convex program — a semidefinite program. It does not get stuck in local minima, and if no common P existed the solver would report infeasibility rather than a bad answer. Note what is not in the program: β. No conditioning factor appears anywhere. That is the point of part three.

The open loop has to be stable already ‖Φ‖P < 1 is a hypothesis, not a result. It says the uncontrolled cycle map must itself contract in the common ruler, which is only possible if ρ(Φ) < 1 to begin with. This benchmark scrapes past at 0.999850. For a plant with a genuinely unstable open cycle, no such P exists, the conditioner still runs and still returns a legal schedule, and only the guarantee is lost.
The bound certifies stability, not speed q = 0.999939 is set by the open-loop endpoint, the slower of the two, because the guarantee has to cover the case β = 0 forever. Taken literally as a rate it would allow 75 000 cycles — 22 seconds — to reduce the error by a factor of 100. The real conditioned loop gets there in six cycles. The bound is a floor under the worst case, not a description of the response.
It is a statement about the linearized model Φ and Acl come from a first-order expansion of 𝒫 around the anchor. Everything certified here is certified for that linear model. Section 15 says exactly what survives for the real nonlinear plant, and what does not.
Part three

Why the β conditioning is still stable

The controller asks for one thing and the plant executes a scaled-down version of it, with a new scale every cycle, chosen by the error itself. That is a time-varying, state-dependent, nonlinear closed loop. It is also, in the linearized model, a straight line segment between two matrices you already have a certificate for.

12

What conditioning does to the loop equation

Recall what happens each cycle. The law asks for δτraw = −Kek; the conditioner computes the largest βk ∈ [0,1] keeping every dwell duration legal; the plant executes βkδτraw. Put that into the linearized cycle model and follow the algebra.

ek+1 = Φek + Γτδτappkthe linearized cycle
δτappk = βkδτrawk = −βkKekwhat actually gets applied
ek+1 = (Φ − βkΓτK)eksubstitute
ΓτK = Φ − Aclfrom the definition of Acl
ek+1 = ((1−βk)Φ + βkAcl)ekrearrange — and that is it
ek+1 = A(βk)ek,    A(β) = (1−β)Φ + βAcl

Read that carefully, because it is the entire structural result. Conditioning does not produce some unknown perturbed matrix. It produces a weighted average of the two matrices part two already certified, with the weight equal to how much of the controller got through. β = 1 is Acl, the full design. β = 0 is Φ, the open loop. Anything in between is a blend.

Why this still needs proving βk is not a constant and not a parameter you get to choose. It is a function of ek — the ratio test reads the error, and returns a number. So the closed loop is nonlinear, and unrolled over cycles it is a product A(βk−1)⋯A(β0) of different matrices, in an order the plant decides. Section 06 showed exactly this arrangement destroying two perfectly stable matrices. Nothing so far rules that out here.
13

Two structural facts that save it

Fact one: it is a segment, and that is a consequence of scaling the whole vector. Suppose the conditioner had instead clipped each of the eight offsets separately — the obvious alternative, and the one the earlier literature used. The applied action would then be −SKek with S = diag(s₁,…,s₈) and each si ∈ [0,1] independent, so the closed-loop matrix would range over

{ Φ − ΓτSK :  s ∈ [0,1]8 }   a polytope with up to 28 = 256 vertices

Certifying that needs a common P for all 256 corner matrices, and there is no reason for one to exist. Multiplying the complete vector by a single scalar collapses those eight independent knobs into one, and 256 vertices into 2. The direction-preservation property that motivated the conditioner on feasibility grounds is the same property that makes it certifiable.

Fact two: the set of contractions is convex. The set {A : ‖A‖P ≤ q} is a ball of a norm — a norm on matrices, since section 04 established that ‖·‖P obeys the triangle inequality and scales properly. Balls of norms are convex. So if both endpoints of a segment lie in that ball, every point of the segment does. There is nothing more to the theorem than those two sentences; the rest is arithmetic.

14

Proposition 2, in full

Proposition 2 — common-Lyapunov contraction

Let P = PT ≻ 0 and suppose ‖Φ‖P ≤ q₀ < 1 and ‖Acl‖P ≤ q₁ < 1. Then every A(β) with β ∈ [0,1] contracts in the same norm by a factor no greater than q = max(q₀, q₁) < 1. The conditioned linearized model is therefore exponentially stable for every sequence {βk} ⊂ [0,1], including the state-dependent ones the conditioner generates.

The proof is two inequalities, and every piece has already been built.

‖A(β)‖P = ‖(1−β)Φ + βAcl‖Pdefinition of the family
           ≤ ‖(1−β)Φ‖P + ‖βAcl‖Ptriangle inequality
           = (1−β)‖Φ‖P + β‖Acl‖Pβ and 1−β are non-negative
           ≤ (1−β)q₀ + βq₁ ≤ max(q₀,q₁) = qa weighted average never exceeds the larger term
‖ek‖P ≤ q‖ek−1‖P ≤ ⋯ ≤ qk‖e0‖Papply it once per cycle

The last line is where the sequence is handled, and it is worth seeing why it is not a sleight of hand. Each cycle is a separate application of the same inequality to whatever matrix was used that cycle. The bound never asks which matrix that was. Consequently the result holds for β constant, for β chosen by the ratio test, for β chosen at random, and for β chosen by an adversary who can see the state and is trying to break the loop. The certificate does not depend on the conditioner being clever, only on its output lying in [0,1].

Where each hypothesis is used The triangle inequality needs ‖·‖P to be a norm — that is why the argument is run on the induced norm rather than directly on the matrix inequality ATPA − P ≺ 0, which is quadratic in A and does not combine so cleanly. The step from one cycle to k cycles needs submultiplicativity. And the fact that both bounds involve the same P is what lets the two endpoint numbers be compared and mixed at all; with two different Lyapunov functions the triangle inequality has nothing to act on.
15

The same claim, against the real run

The proposition is about the linearized model. The paper also runs the exact nonlinear plant, 100 cycles of genuine matrix-exponential propagation, from an initial error far outside the range where linearization was validated. That run is what the next figure shows.

The first cycle is the interesting one. The raw aggressive action there asked for a dwell of −43.045 µs, which would have put two switching instants out of order — a schedule the converter cannot execute at all. Conditioning scaled the whole vector by 0.293978, the largest factor that keeps every interval at or above the assumed 3 µs floor, and the loop kept contracting through it. By cycle 3 the ratio test stops binding and the loop is running the design as drawn.

What is proved, and what is not — read this before quoting the result

Proved, for the linearized model: exponential stability under every possible conditioning-factor sequence, by Proposition 2.

Proved, for the exact nonlinear plant, locally: because the nominal schedule keeps a strict dwell margin and the raw law asks for nothing at zero error, some neighbourhood of the anchor lies entirely inside the region where the raw action is already legal. Inside it β = 1 identically, the conditioner never acts, the Jacobian of the exact closed-loop map is Acl, and since that matrix is Schur the anchor — and the phase-locked periodic trajectory through it — is locally exponentially stable.

Not proved: global stability of the nonlinear plant. Outside that neighbourhood, Proposition 2 still covers arbitrary conditioning, but only for the linear model; nobody has bounded the nonlinear remainder over the large-error region. The 100-cycle run above is evidence for this benchmark, not a theorem, and the 3 µs floor is a simulation assumption rather than a hardware figure.

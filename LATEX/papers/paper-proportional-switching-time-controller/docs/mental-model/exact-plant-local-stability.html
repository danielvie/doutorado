01 · Separate the objects

There is one ideal plant, described at two levels

The exact one-cycle map

Start from the current state, apply the controller's actual dwell durations, and propagate each mode through the whole cycle.

This keeps the timing dependence. It gives the original model's next cycle-start state without a Taylor approximation.

The linearized one-cycle model

Approximate how the next error changes when the current error and switching offsets are small.

This is the simpler matrix model used in the common-P proof. It drops higher-order terms.

Exact next error = linearized prediction + omitted higher-order terms

The common-P argument acts on the linearized prediction. For the exact feedback map, we must also account for the omitted terms. A certificate for one equation does not automatically certify another equation.

“Exact” does not mean hardware-perfect. It means exact propagation of the paper's ideal switched-affine equations. Quantization, delays, estimation errors, and parameter uncertainty are still outside those equations.

02 · Where the nonlinear terms come from

Linear mode equations do not make timing feedback linear

Within each mode, we know how to propagate the state. But the result depends on how long the mode lasts. When feedback makes that duration depend on the error, the error enters the timing-dependent propagation too.

Fixed switching times

The same propagation matrices act every cycle. The one-cycle state map is affine. Subtract the reference anchor, and its constant term cancels.

eₖ₊₁ = Φeₖ exactly

Error-dependent switching times

The error changes the durations used inside the propagation. Matrix exponentials now depend on the error.

eₖ₊₁ = A(βₖ)eₖ + remainder

A one-state example you can inspect

Use a one-second cycle. In the first mode, dx/dt = −x + 1. In the second, dx/dt = −x − 1. Normally, each mode lasts half a second. The state is dimensionless, and time is measured in seconds.

With feedback, request a switching offset of δτ = −0.2e seconds. Both dwell durations must stay above 0.15 seconds. The same maximal-scaling rule as in the paper enforces this bound. This is a teaching model, not a converter parameter choice.

The exact equations behind Laboratory 1

A mode with constant command u propagates as x(t+d) = u + [x(t)−u] exp(−d). Compose the two modes, with the switch at τ, to obtain

F(x,τ) = exp(−1)x + 2 exp(τ−1) − 1 − exp(−1).

The nominal anchor solves F(x*,0.5) = x*, giving x* = −tanh(0.25), about −0.244919. Set e = x−x* and τ = 0.5+δτ. The exact error map is

eₖ₊₁ = exp(−1)eₖ + 2 exp(−0.5)[exp(δτₖ)−1].

The local approximation replaces exp(δτ)−1 by δτ. With δτ = −0.2βe, the conditioned linearized prediction is [exp(−1)−0.4 exp(−0.5)β]e. The remainder is 2 exp(−0.5)[exp(δτ)−1−δτ].

The prediction is linear for a fixed β. Because the controller selects β from the error, the dashed curve can bend where conditioning activates. That state dependence is retained in both calculations; the propagation remainder is what distinguishes them.

Near zero error, β = 1 and halving the error reduces this remainder by roughly a factor of four. That is what “second order” means here. The nonlinear terms have not been removed from the exact model.

The available timing margin is 0.35 seconds. For a nonzero raw offset, β = min(1, 0.35/|δτraw|). At zero offset we take β = 1. Thus both dwells remain at least 0.15 seconds.

The fixed schedule gives an exact linear error relation. Timing feedback generally does not, even when each mode has simple affine dynamics.

03 · What the proof can transfer to the exact map

Near the anchor, the omitted terms are small enough

There are two reasons the paper can make a local statement about the exact feedback map.

  1. Conditioning is inactive sufficiently close to the anchor. The nominal dwells have strict positive margins. Small errors produce small raw offsets, which fit inside those margins. Therefore β = 1 there.
  2. The stable linear part dominates the small nonlinear remainder. In that neighborhood, the exact map is smooth and its Jacobian is Acl. The endpoint certificate proves that this Jacobian is Schur stable. The discrete-time linearization theorem gives local exponential stability of the exact map.

Jacobian, in this paragraph

The derivative of the exact next-error map at e = 0. It tells us the first-order effect of a small current error.

In one dimension it is a slope. With several states it is a matrix of slopes.

Schur stable, in this paragraph

Every eigenvalue of that discrete-time matrix has magnitude below one. The corresponding linear system converges to zero.

This is different from the Schur complement, which is a matrix manipulation used in the proof.

Local means that a sufficiently small neighborhood exists from which trajectories stay nearby and converge. This section does not calculate its size. It does not say that every point in the dwell-feasible region C lies in it.

Feasibility and convergence answer different questions. C says “the raw schedule is allowed now.” A region of attraction says “the trajectory will approach the reference.” They are not interchangeable.

Why the polynomial example is locally stable

For |e| ≤ 0.25, |0.5e+e²| ≤ 0.75|e|. So this interval is invariant and every trajectory starting there decays. But e = 0.5 is another fixed point. For e > 0.5, the next error exceeds the current one, and repeated application grows without bound. The behavior away from zero cannot be read from the derivative at zero alone.

The common-P proof in the paper covers the entire linearized family. This polynomial example has only one linearized matrix, 0.5, but that is enough to illustrate the missing logical step. The linear stability certificate does not certify the omitted nonlinear term at arbitrary errors.

04 · What “converges to the anchor” means

The samples become constant. The waveform does not.

The anchor is one point on the reference waveform, always sampled at the same phase: the beginning of a cycle. It is not a physical equilibrium at which the plant stops moving.

Two independent distinctions

Exact versus linearized

Which equations are we using? Exact propagation retains the full timing dependence. Linearization keeps the first-order terms near the reference.

Local versus global

Which initial states does the guarantee cover? Local means a neighborhood. Global means all states in the stated mathematical model.

There is no contradiction in a stronger scope statement for open loop and a faster response with feedback. In this benchmark, open loop already converges. The feedback simulations show faster convergence while the conditioner keeps the requested dwell durations feasible. The nonlinear guarantee for that feedback remains local.

Your common-P reasoning remains useful. The missing question is: “Does the equation I certified equal the exact cycle map, or only approximate it?”

Review round 2 · The missing timing convention

“Near the anchor” refers to the cycle-start state

“i dont get the part where near the anchor, the error is small. is the anchor the starting point of the limit cycle? if not, then i was under the impression that the error might not be small”

Yes: the anchor is the chosen start-of-cycle point on the reference periodic trajectory. A closed cycle has no intrinsic starting point, so we select a phase and call its reference state x*. That is not necessarily where the actual transient starts.

Your question identifies a timing convention that the first explanation did not emphasize enough. The controller's error is evaluated only at the start of a cycle:

eₖ = x(kT) − x*.

Thus “near the anchor” means if the actual state at a cycle start is near the reference state for that same phase. It is a condition, not an assertion that the state stays near this one point throughout the cycle, or that every initial error is small.

Three different situations

  • At a cycle start, the actual state is close to x*. The sampled error is small, so sufficiently small timing corrections fit within the dwell margins.
  • Inside a perfectly tracked cycle, the state is far from x*. This is allowed. It should be compared with the moving reference at that phase, not with the fixed anchor.
  • The transient starts far from x*. Then e₀ can be large and conditioning may activate. The local argument does not claim otherwise.

The controller computes the timing action at the cycle start and applies the scheduled offsets during that cycle. It does not continuously recompute the error as x(t) − x* while the reference moves.

Review round 3 · What is actually certified?

The example converges, but the local proof does not certify its whole transient

“how are we proving that the exact one-cycle map is stable if the system can start far from the reference anchor. actually, the example itself activates the conditioning on the first 3 cycles. … if your exact one-cycle map only proves the stability close to the reference and cannot prove it on the start of the system … then whats the point?”
This objection identifies a real difference between the theorem's scope and the experiment's scope. The current analysis does not prove nonlinear convergence from the example's initial state. The exact simulation demonstrates that this trajectory converges.

The earlier explanations described why local stability holds but did not make this limitation prominent enough. Knowing that a sufficiently small stable neighborhood exists does not prove that a distant trajectory reaches it. “The example is stable” is supported as observed convergence of the simulated trajectory; it is not, by itself, a proof covering other initial states or disturbances.

Stopping the conditioning does not close this gap

At cycle 3 the simulation reports β = 1. That means the raw request is dwell-feasible at that state. It does not identify entry into a certified nonlinear invariant neighborhood. C is a raw-action feasibility region, not automatically a nonlinear region of attraction. The paper has not computed the local neighborhood's size or certified the trajectory's entry into it.

Nor can the exact open-loop result be substituted for a feedback proof. The nominal schedule is already stable in the ideal model, but adding state-dependent timing changes the cycle map. The open-loop guarantee does not automatically transfer to that map.

What the current contribution is, and is not

The present results support solver-free dwell feasibility, a common quadratic certificate for the conditioned linearized family, local exact stability, and simulated convergence acceleration. They do not support a claim that the nonlinear conditioned controller is proved stable throughout the demonstrated large-error transient.

The local exact result needs a Schur Acl and strict nominal dwell margins. It does not need the entire common-P interval theorem. The value of the common-P theorem is its stronger treatment of changing conditioning factors in the linearized family, rather than only testing the raw endpoint. That remains a linearized result.

If the goal is to certify this initial condition

One possible route is to certify a nonlinear invariant neighborhood around the anchor, then rigorously verify that the exact transient from the chosen initial state reaches that neighborhood while respecting the dwell constraints. This would cover that initial state without claiming global nonlinear stability. A broader route is a nonlinear Lyapunov or remainder-bound analysis covering a region that contains the initial state.

Neither route has been completed in the current manuscript. A finite floating-point trajectory that appears to enter the neighborhood, without a certified set and verified membership, is not that missing proof.

What the Schur stability proof needs

Your explanation

“To prove the stability we need to have a P where V is positive definite and that in the Schur complement the matrices are negative definite.”

This is correct for the paper's linearized family, provided the same P works at both endpoints. The remaining step is to connect the matrix inequality to the change in V along a trajectory. The extra symbols in the circled passage quantify this decrease; they are not additional design requirements.

Scope: the conditioned linearized model zk+1 = A(βk)zk, with A(β) = (1−β)A0 + βA1 and β in [0,1]. This does not give a global stability guarantee for the exact nonlinear plant.

The complete chain

For P ≻ 0, the block with diagonal entries −P and −P−1 is negative definite exactly when A(β)TPA(β) − P ≺ 0. Substituting the dynamics into V gives the missing connection:

V(zk+1) − V(zk)
= zkT[A(βk)TPA(βk) − P]zk < 0
for zk ≠ 0.

The same V works at every cycle, even when β changes. Because the matrix family is continuous on the closed interval [0,1], its strict decrease has a common positive margin. More precisely, the quadratic decrease has a positive minimum over β in [0,1] and error directions on the unit sphere. Together with P ≻ 0, this establishes uniform exponential stability.

What the circled notation adds

η names a guaranteed decrease margin. λmax(P) converts between V and the ordinary squared error magnitude. Their ratio makes an explicit contraction argument possible. The strict common matrix inequalities already imply the existence of such a contraction; the main proof need not display its formula.

Editorial recommendation: retain the displayed identity connecting the dynamics to V, then state that continuity on [0,1] makes the decrease uniform. Omit the explicit eigenvalue-based contraction formula. The numerical SDP can retain a margin to check strict inequalities in floating-point arithmetic.

Claim review and evidence

  • Supported: P ≻ 0 makes V positive definite, and negative definite Schur blocks establish strict decrease.
  • Necessary qualification: both endpoint tests use the same P. Separate endpoint Lyapunov matrices do not supply this proof.
  • Missing connection, not a misconception: the identity above explains why the matrix inequality means V decreases along the actual linearized trajectory.

Round 2: separate certificates versus a common certificate

“if A0 passes with P0 and A1 passes with a different P1, then we cannot say the system is stable. Since we cannot garantee they belong to the same convex set. They each have a convex set that could be inside a non convex set”

The conclusion is supported, with a correction to the reason. Separate certificates establish stability of the two fixed endpoint systems. They do not establish stability of every intermediate matrix or every sequence. They also do not prove instability, or prove that a common P cannot exist.

The Schur blocks both belong to the same convex cone of symmetric negative-definite matrices. Their convex combination remains negative definite. The problem is its structure: with different P matrices, its diagonal blocks become −[(1−β)P₀+βP₁] and −[(1−β)P₀⁻¹+βP₁⁻¹]. Generally, the second is not the negative inverse of the matrix in the first. It is therefore not the block used in our proof for one common P and A(β).

Geometrically, one common P means one family of ellipsoidal level sets that both endpoint maps contract. Separate P matrices give separate measures of error. Decrease according to V₀ and decrease according to V₁ cannot simply be joined into decrease of one function. Whether the union of their ellipsoids is convex is not the test.

An exact counterexample

A₀ = [[0.5, 2], [0, 0.5]],   A₁ = [[0.5, 0], [2, 0.5]]
Both endpoints have eigenvalues 0.5 and 0.5.
The midpoint is [[0.5, 1], [1, 0.5]], with eigenvalues 1.5 and −0.5.

Each endpoint is Schur stable, but the midpoint is unstable. This example concerns illustrative matrices, not the converter benchmark. Explicit endpoint certificates are P₀ = [[4/3, 16/9], [16/9, 356/27]] and P₁ with its diagonal entries exchanged. Both are positive definite and satisfy Pᵢ − AᵢᵀPᵢAᵢ = I. These identities can be checked with rational arithmetic.

SECTION 3  ·  EXPLAINED FROM SCRATCH

The Dwell-Time Conditioner

A controller asks for a switching schedule that would run one interval for −43.045 µs. Negative time. Here is the one-line repair that makes it legal without changing what the controller was trying to do — and why that particular repair, and no other, comes with a stability proof.

00

The machine, in one paragraph

A DC–DC power converter is a box of transistors that chops a DC voltage on and off very fast, so that the average output lands where you want it. The chopping pattern repeats forever. One repetition is called a cycle.

In this paper's benchmark the cycle lasts 286 µs and is divided into 9 intervals. During each interval the transistors hold one fixed configuration, and the circuit obeys one fixed set of differential equations. At the boundary between two intervals the transistors flip, and the equations change instantly.

Repeating that pattern makes the voltages and currents trace out a closed loop in state space — a limit cycle. That loop is the target. The control problem is: when the converter drifts off the loop, nudge it back.

The one unusual idea The controller does not change any voltage or any transistor. Its only power is to decide when the switches flip — to slide the interval boundaries slightly earlier or later. That is the whole actuator. Everything below is about the consequences of having such a strange, restricted knob.
01

What can move, and what cannot

Nine intervals need ten boundaries. Number them t₀ … t₉.

The first and last are welded in place. t₀ = 0 and t₉ = 286 µs, always. That is what "fixed period" means, and it is not a stylistic choice — power converters are legally and thermally required to switch at a constant frequency, so the cycle length is untouchable.

That leaves the 8 interior boundaries free to slide. The amount each one slides is called its offset, written δτ₁ … δτ₈. Stack them into a vector δτ. This vector is the controller's entire output.

02

The trap: durations are gaps, not things

Here is the subtlety that makes the whole problem interesting. The controller moves boundaries, but the physical constraint applies to durations. And a duration is not an independent quantity — it is the gap between two boundaries:

dᵢ = tᵢ − tᵢ₋₁

So when you slide boundary i later by some amount, two durations change at once, in opposite directions:

interval i  (the one ending at that boundary)   gets longer
interval i+1 (the one starting at that boundary)  gets shorter

by exactly the same amount. In the paper this bookkeeping is the matrix D of equation (14), and the statement δd = D·δτ means nothing more than "take differences of neighbouring offsets."

Consequence worth pausing on Because every offset gives away exactly what it takes, the nine durations always add up to 286 µs — no matter what the controller asks for, however crazy. The period can never be broken. (The paper writes this as 1ᵀD = 0.) Keep this in mind: it is why the repair in Step 05 is safe.
03

The hard limit, and how the controller blows through it

Real transistors cannot be switched on and immediately off. Gate drivers need time, the device needs to finish turning on, and measurements need a settled window. So every interval carries a floor:

every dᵢ  ≥  dmin = 3 µs

The controller — an LQR, designed in the usual way — has never heard of this floor. It is a matrix multiply, δτraw = −K·e: take the error, multiply, emit eight numbers. Nothing in that computation can refuse.

Start the converter far from its target loop, at the initial state the paper uses, and ask the aggressive LQR what it wants. Its most extreme request is on the eighth boundary:

boundary 8 sits at  263.828 µs
controller asks for  +65.217 µs  (slide it later)
that would put it at  329.045 µs

But boundary 9 is welded at 286 µs. The ninth interval is the gap between them, so its requested duration is

286.000 − 329.045  =  −43.045 µs

This is not "a bit too aggressive." A negative duration means boundary 8 has jumped over boundary 9. The intervals are now out of order. There is no schedule here to execute, correct, or approximate — the request is not a schedule at all.

And it is not just one interval On this cycle the raw request drives four of the nine intervals below the floor at once — intervals 2, 6, 7 and 9 — and every one of them to a negative duration. Any fix has to handle all of them simultaneously. You will see the full audit in Step 06.
04

The obvious fix, and why it quietly ruins everything

The reflex is to clamp each number. Boundary 8 wants +65.2 µs but only +19.2 µs is legal, so give it +19.2. Do that for each of the eight offsets independently. This is what a saturation block does, and it is what the earlier literature's standalone LQR comparison did.

It fails twice.

Failure one — it does not actually work

Step 02 showed that each duration depends on two adjacent offsets. Clamp offset 8 to protect interval 9, and you have just changed interval 8 as well. The violation moves next door instead of disappearing. You can chase it around the cycle and it does not converge.

Failure two — the real one

Clamping each component by a different amount rotates the vector. The eight numbers that come out are no longer proportional to the eight numbers that went in; they point somewhere else entirely.

That matters because everything you know about the controller is a statement about K. You proved Acl = Φ − Γ·K is stable. But the plant is no longer receiving −K·e. It is receiving some other vector, produced by a nonlinear, state-dependent, eight-way clamp. You have silently swapped in a different controller and you cannot write down what it is.

Now compare the alternative. Keep the direction and give up only the length: multiply all eight numbers by one single scalar β between 0 and 1. The vector shrinks along its own ray. It still points exactly where the controller pointed.

05

One dial for the whole vector

So here is the entire method. The controller emits its eight raw offsets. Before they reach the transistors, multiply all of them by one number β:

δτapplied  =  β · δτraw ,    β ∈ [0, 1]

Think of a mixing desk. Clipping is muting individual instruments until the room is quiet enough — you get silence in the right places and a different piece of music. Scaling is the master fader: same performance, turned down.

Three properties come free, and each one matters:

The direction survives. Multiplying by a positive scalar cannot rotate a vector. The plant receives a shortened version of precisely what the controller asked for.

The period survives. From Step 02, the durations always sum to 286 µs. Scaling every offset scales every duration change, and zero times any scalar is still zero.

The mode order survives. At β = 0 nothing moves at all, which is trivially legal. Turning β up slides the boundaries continuously, so there is always some range near zero where nothing crosses.

06

Finding the largest β that works

You want the biggest β you can get away with — turning the fader down further than necessary throws away control authority you were entitled to use. Finding it takes one pass over nine numbers.

For each interval, ask two questions.

Is it shrinking? If the request makes this interval longer, it can never violate a minimum. It imposes no limit at all. Skip it. (Three of the nine intervals are in this category.)

If it is shrinking, how much room does it have? Its margin is how far it can be squeezed before hitting the floor: margin = nominal − 3 µs. If the full request would shrink it by s, then the largest legal fraction of that request is the ratio margin ⁄ s.

Each shrinking interval hands you one ceiling. Every ceiling must hold at once, so take the smallest — and cap at 1, since you never want to amplify the controller:

β = min ( 1 ,  min over shrinking intervals of  marginᵢ ⁄ shrinkᵢ )

That is equation (22). Here is the arithmetic on the real cycle, all nine rows:

Interval Nominal Direction Raw duration Margin Shrink Ceiling on β
Margin = nominal − 3 µs Ceiling = margin ⁄ shrink Highlighted row = the binding constraint

Six intervals shrink, so six ceilings. The tightest is interval 9's, and that single row decides the whole cycle:

β = min(1, 4.0076, 0.8478, 8.1942, 0.8387, 0.5761, 0.2940) = 0.293978

Nine comparisons. No optimiser, no matrix inverse, no iteration — which is the point of calling it solver-free. A microcontroller does this between switching cycles without noticing.

Why the answer lands on exactly 3.000 µs Apply β = 0.293978 and interval 9 becomes 22.172 − 0.293978 × 65.217 = 3.000 µs — precisely the floor, not a micron above it. That is not a coincidence, it is the definition of maximal. The binding constraint is pushed exactly to its limit, because if it landed above the floor you could have turned the fader up further. Every other interval ends up with room to spare.

This is Proposition 1 in the paper. Its proof is three lines and you have now seen all of them: growing intervals impose nothing, each shrinking interval imposes one ceiling, intersecting the ceilings with [0,1] gives the formula — so the formula is simultaneously legal (every constraint holds) and maximal (no larger value does).

07

The payoff: why scaling was worth insisting on

Everything so far is a sensible clamp. This step is why it is a result.

Ask what the closed loop actually does over one cycle. Two matrices describe the extremes:

Φ — the open-loop cycle map. Where the error goes if the controller does nothing.

Acl = Φ − Γ·K — the full-correction cycle map. Where the error goes when the controller's request is applied in full.

Now substitute β·δτraw into the cycle model and the algebra collapses to one line:

A(β)  =  (1 − β)·Φ  +  β·Acl

Read that carefully, because it is the whole paper. Conditioning cannot produce an arbitrary matrix. Whatever β the plant generates, the resulting closed loop lands on the straight line segment between two matrices you already know — and nowhere else. β = 0 is one endpoint, β = 1 is the other, everything in between is a weighted blend.

Clipping gives you nothing like this. Its applied vector leaves the ray, so the effective closed loop leaves the segment and scatters into a set you cannot describe.

08

Two checks that cover infinitely many cases

To prove a discrete-time loop is stable you find an energy function that always decreases. Here it is a quadratic form ‖x‖P, set by a matrix P you compute once, offline. If a matrix shrinks that energy every cycle, the error must decay.

Suppose you find a single P that works for both endpoints:

‖Φ‖P = 0.999939 < 1    and    ‖Acl‖P = 0.979743 < 1

Then the triangle inequality does the rest in a single step:

‖A(β)‖P ≤ (1−β)·‖Φ‖P + β·‖Acl‖P ≤ 0.999939 < 1

A weighted average of two numbers below 1 is below 1. That is the entire proof of Proposition 2.

And here is what makes it genuinely useful: it holds for any sequence of β values whatsoever. The plant may produce 0.29, 0.45, 0.78, 1, 1, 1, … in a state-dependent way nobody can predict in advance. It does not matter. Every term in that sequence maps to a point on the certified segment, so every cycle contracts. You never have to model when conditioning fires.

Read the small print The certificate needs ‖Φ‖P < 1 — the uncontrolled converter must already be stable on its own. Here it barely is, at 0.999939. So the certified bound is dominated by the open-loop endpoint, not the controller. It is a worst case covering the hypothetical "β stays near zero forever," not a prediction of observed speed. Once β returns to 1 the loop actually contracts at 0.568 per cycle. If a plant's open loop were unstable, the conditioner would still run and still produce legal schedules — only the guarantee would be lost.
09

What happens on the actual converter

Simulated on the exact nonlinear switched model — no linear approximation anywhere — from a large initial error, against two honest opponents: the same aggressive LQR is compared with a conservative LQR detuned until it never violates the floor by itself, and with open loop.

Three things are worth extracting from that picture.

The repair is not a handicap. The conditioned aggressive law settles in 6 cycles against the conservative law's 20. Detuning a controller until it is always feasible costs more than letting it be aggressive and scaling back only when reality intervenes.

Conditioning is rare. It acts on 3 cycles out of 100. It is an emergency response to a large error, not a permanent part of the loop.

The system spends its life in the good region. From cycle 3 onward β = 1, the conditioner is dormant, and the plant is running the plain designed controller with all its ordinary guarantees.

10

What this does not prove

The paper is careful here, and a fair reading should be too.

The Lyapunov certificate is about the linearised model. The segment argument lives in the first-order cycle model, not the true nonlinear switched plant.

The exact nonlinear guarantee is local only. Near the target loop the nominal margins are wide, so β = 1 always and the conditioner never acts — which is exactly why the guarantee holds there. Outside that neighbourhood the 100-cycle simulation is evidence for this benchmark, not a theorem.

The conditioner is deliberately dumb. It cannot look ahead, and it cannot pick a different direction when the requested one is nearly blocked — if the raw vector points almost straight at a wall, β collapses toward zero and you get almost no correction, even though some other direction might have served. Predictive control would re-plan. This will not. That is the price of having no solver.

The 3 µs floor is an assumption. It is not hardware-calibrated. Timer quantisation, gate delays, dead time and implementation margin are all absent, as are state estimation error, model uncertainty, and any experiment.

\title[Dwell-conditioned switching-time feedback]{Dwell-Conditioned Cycle-to-Cycle Switching-Time Feedback for Mode-Dependent Switched-Affine Systems}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Daniel} \sur{Vieira}}\email{danielvie@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Roberto Kawakami Harrop} \sur{Galv\~ao}}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Systems and Control}, \orgname{Instituto Tecnol\'ogico de Aeron\'autica}, \orgaddress{\city{S\~ao Jos\'e dos Campos}, \state{S\~ao Paulo}, \country{Brazil}}}

\abstract{
We propose proportional switching-time feedback for switched-affine systems to track a prescribed periodic trajectory rather than a constant average operating point. The controller requests offsets for the interior switching instants, and a solver-free conditioner uniformly scales these offsets whenever the requested schedule violates the minimum dwell durations. This enforces the dwell constraints while preserving the action direction, mode order, and cycle period. An offline common quadratic Lyapunov certificate establishes uniform exponential stability of the conditioned linearized model even when the conditioning factor changes between cycles. The certificate is verified for a three-cell DC--DC converter, where simulations of the exact plant show faster convergence than conservative feedback and open-loop operation while respecting the dwell constraints.
}

\keywords{switched-affine systems, periodic trajectories, switching-time control, dwell-time conditioning, common quadratic Lyapunov function, semidefinite programming}

\section{Introduction}

Power converters control voltage and current by moving through a sequence of switch configurations. The switching pattern must respect electrical limits, switching frequency, a minimum dwell duration for transition, and a short computation time \cite{karamanakos2020mpcPowerElectronics}. Keeping the individual configurations explicit gives a hybrid-affine or switched-affine model \cite{albea2021hybridAffinePwm}. When repeating the pattern, then it produces a periodic trajectory rather than a constant state, and controlling such trajectories is an established problem for switched-affine systems \cite{egidio2020,xu2024fcsLimitCycle}.

Predictive control tackles the problem by solving a constrained optimization problem online. Recent work reduces the online burden through Laguerre parameterization, dedicated fixed-frequency solvers, and candidate pruning \cite{saeed2022laguerre,yang2022fixed,augustine2023mpc}. Controllers also differ in what they may change. State-dependent switching laws choose the active subsystem \cite{egidio2022rank}, where switching-time control keeps an ordered sequence and moves its transition instants. We take this same approach by changing the transition instants while keeping all other parameters fixed. At each cycle start, the error is computed and the offsets applied during that cycle, with no online solver.

Fixed-period switching-time control shifts only the interior switching instants without changing the mode sequence or cycle period. It targets a reference periodic trajectory rather than an equilibrium point. Patino et al. studied how individual switching instants affect mode-dependent cyclic systems and used predictive control to track an optimal periodic trajectory \cite{patino2010}. Marcolino et al. regulated the cycle-start state toward a target point on the periodic trajectory, referred to in this paper as the cycle anchor. For a fixed-period linear model with switched actuator levels, they used a one-cycle model to compute switching-instant corrections through discrete LQR and dwell-constrained predictive control \cite{marcolino2021}. Shifting a switching instant makes the preceding dwell duration larger and the following one shorter, coupling their minimum-dwell constraints. Marcolino et al. enforce these constraints through online optimization in their predictive controller. Their LQR method instead clips each adjustment separately to meet the dwell limits. This can change the direction of the requested adjustments and may shift the final switching instant \cite{marcolino2021}. Preserving the direction of the control action through control allocation still requires online optimization and does not address fixed-period dwell constraints \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. The remaining problem is to compute the largest feasible scale without an online solver. The scale must preserve the action direction and mode order without changing the cycle period.

To make fixed-period switching-time feedback dwell-feasible, we scale every requested offset by the same factor, taking the smallest of $1$ and the bounds imposed by the $N$ dwell constraints. We find this largest feasible factor in $O(N)$ time without an online solver, while scaling the full raw offset vector preserves its direction, the mode order, and the cycle period. We assess convergence separately with an offline common quadratic Lyapunov certificate, which establishes uniform exponential stability of the conditioned linearized model even when the factor changes between cycles. The conditioner makes each finite timing request dwell-feasible, but the certificate does not guarantee convergence of the exact nonlinear plant from arbitrary errors. \Cref{sec:model} derives the cycle model, \cref{sec:conditioning} develops the conditioner, \cref{sec:stability} presents the stability analysis, and \cref{sec:case-study} presents the converter study.

\section{Problem formulation and one-cycle model}
\label{sec:model}

To compute the timing action that the conditioner will scale, we first need a model of how switching-time adjustments affect tracking. With the mode sequence and cycle period fixed, we derive a linearized relation between the tracking error at the start of a cycle, the timing offsets applied during that cycle, and the error at the start of the next cycle. We then use this model to define the raw proportional feedback law.

\subsection{Plant, reference trajectory, and timing coordinates}

Consider the switched-affine plant
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vect{x}}(t)=A_{\sigma(t)}\vect{x}(t)+\vect{b}_{\sigma(t)},
    \qquad \vect{x}(t)\in\R^n.
    \label{eq:plant}
\end{equation}
Every cycle executes the sequence $(q_1,\ldots,q_N)$. Its nominal cycle boundaries and dwell durations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    0&=\bar t_0<\bar t_1<\cdots<\bar t_N=T,\\
    \bar d_i&=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The given reference schedule generates $\bar{\vect{x}}(t)$ with anchor $\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=\bar{\vect{x}}(T)$. Define $\vect{x}_k=\vect{x}(kT)$ and $\vect{e}_k=\vect{x}_k-\bar{\vect{x}}(0)$. The target is periodic motion, not a constant average operating point.

The controller moves only the $N-1$ interior switching instants, keeping the cycle endpoints fixed, as illustrated in \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(a):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    t_{k,i}&=\bar t_i+\delta\tau_{k,i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\\
    \delta\tau_{k,0}&=\delta\tau_{k,N}=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
During cycle $k$, mode $q_i$ is active from $kT+t_{k,i-1}$ to $kT+t_{k,i}$, so its dwell duration is $d_{k,i}=t_{k,i}-t_{k,i-1}$. Moving a boundary transfers time between its neighboring intervals. In \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(b), delaying instant 8 by \SI{12}{\micro\second} lengthens dwell 8 by \SI{12}{\micro\second} and shortens dwell 9 by the same amount; the other dwells remain unchanged.

These transfers preserve the total period $T$, but a sufficiently large shift can reverse the boundary order, as \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(c) illustrates. We therefore require every applied dwell to satisfy $d_{k,i}\ge d_{\min}$, where $d_{\min}>0$ is the minimum allowed dwell duration.

\subsection{Linear Equation}

With a constant auxiliary coordinate, the augmented state and matrix are
\begin{equation}
    \X(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}(t)\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \widetilde A_q=
    \begin{bmatrix}A_q&\vect{b}_q\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&0\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:augmented-generator}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
    \dot\X(t)=
    \begin{bmatrix}A_{\sigma(t)}\vect{x}(t)+\vect{b}_{\sigma(t)}\\0\end{bmatrix}
    =\widetilde A_{\sigma(t)}\X(t).
\]
Within one cycle, the index $k$ is omitted and time runs from $t_0=\bar t_0=0$ to $t_N=\bar t_N=T$. The interval quantities are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta t_i&=t_i-t_{i-1},\\
    \Delta\bar t_i&=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1},\\
    \Delta t_i&=\Delta\bar t_i+\delta t_i,\qquad F_i=\widetilde A_{q_i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:duration-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta t_i=d_{k,i}$, $\Delta\bar t_i=\bar d_i$, and $\delta t_i=\delta d_{k,i}$ is a duration change. The interval propagation gives
\begin{equation}
    \X(t_i)=e^{F_i\Delta t_i}\X(t_{i-1}).
\end{equation}
Over the full cycle,
\begin{equation}
    \X(t_N)=e^{F_N\Delta t_N}e^{F_{N-1}\Delta t_{N-1}}
    \cdots e^{F_1\Delta t_1}\X(t_0).
    \label{eq:exact-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
We can rewrite the terms
\begin{equation}
    \phi_i=e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}.
    \label{eq:nominal-interval-transition}
\end{equation}
So \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-cycle} becomes
\begin{equation}
    \bar\X(t_N)=\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
    \label{eq:nominal-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
The augmented error satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t)&=\X(t)-\bar\X(t),\\
    \X(t_0)&=\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
With $\vect{e}(t_0)=[\vect{e}_k^{\mathsf T}\ 0]^{\mathsf T}\in\R^{n+1}$, the terminal error is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)={}&\X(t_N)-\bar\X(t_N)\\
    ={}&e^{F_N(\Delta\bar t_N+\delta t_N)}
    e^{F_{N-1}(\Delta\bar t_{N-1}+\delta t_{N-1})}\cdots\\
    &\quad e^{F_1(\Delta\bar t_1+\delta t_1)}
    \bigl(\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0)\bigr)\\
    &-\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:exact-augmented-error}
\end{equation}

The first-order expansion about the nominal duration gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}e^{F_i s}&=F_i e^{F_i s},\\
    e^{F_i(\Delta\bar t_i+\delta t_i)}
    &=e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}
      +F_i e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}\,\delta t_i
      +\mathcal O(\delta t_i^2)\\
    &=\phi_i+F_i\phi_i\,\delta t_i+\mathcal O(\delta t_i^2)\\
    &\doteq\phi_i+F_i\phi_i\,\delta t_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:interval-first-order}
\end{equation}
Here $\doteq$ denotes equality up to first order. Substitution into \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-error} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq{}&
    (\phi_N+F_N\phi_N\,\delta t_N)\\
    &\times(\phi_{N-1}+F_{N-1}\phi_{N-1}\,\delta t_{N-1})\cdots\\
    &\times(\phi_1+F_1\phi_1\,\delta t_1)
    \bigl(\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0)\bigr)\\
    &-\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:product-before-expansion}
\end{equation}
The first-order product expansion in the duration changes is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq{}&
    \phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\\
    &+\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\vect{e}(t_0)\\
    &+\Bigl[F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\,\delta t_N\\
    &\qquad{}+\phi_NF_{N-1}\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\,\delta t_{N-1}\\
    &\qquad{}+\cdots\\
    &\qquad{}+\phi_N\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\,\delta t_1\Bigr]\\
    &\quad\times\bigl(\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0)\bigr)\\
    &-\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ordered-product-first-order}
\end{equation}
After cancellation of the nominal terms and omission of the products $\delta t_i\vect{e}(t_0)$ \eqref{eq:ordered-product-first-order} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq{}&
    \phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\vect{e}(t_0)\\
    &+\phi_N\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_1\\
    &+\phi_N\cdots\phi_3F_2\phi_2\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_2\\
    &+\cdots\\
    &+F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_N.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:expanded-terminal-error}
\end{equation}

Define the duration-change vector, coefficient matrix, and complete nominal transition as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \delta\vect{t}&=[\delta t_1\ \delta t_2\ \cdots\ \delta t_N]^{\mathsf T},\\
    \Gamma&=\Bigl[\,\phi_N\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\bar\X(t_0),\\
    &\qquad\phi_N\cdots\phi_3F_2\phi_2\phi_1\bar\X(t_0),\ \ldots,\\
    &\qquad F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\Bigr],\\
    \Phi&=\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:augmented-dwell-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
With $\Gamma\in\R^{(n+1)\times N}$ and $\Phi\in\R^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}$, \eqref{eq:expanded-terminal-error} becomes the linear equation
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq\Phi\vect{e}(t_0)+\Gamma\delta\vect{t}.
    \label{eq:augmented-error-linearization}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Switching-time model}

The controller moves switching instants, whereas \eqref{eq:augmented-error-linearization} uses interval durations. The model therefore needs to relate these offsets to the physical error at the next cycle.

Each offset transfers time between adjacent intervals, as in \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(b). Since $\delta d_{k,i}=\delta\tau_{k,i}-\delta\tau_{k,i-1}$ and the endpoint offsets are zero,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \ddwell_k&=D\dtau_k,\\
    D&=\begin{bmatrix}
         1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\
        -1 & 1 & \ddots & \vdots\\
         0 & -1 & \ddots & 0\\
         \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 1\\
         0 & \cdots & 0 & -1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in\R^{N\times(N-1)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:incidence-map}
\end{equation}
The relation $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$ ensures that the total period is unchanged.

The matrix $\Pi$ removes the auxiliary coordinate, while its transpose appends a zero:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi=\begin{bmatrix}I_n&\vect{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \Pi^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}I_n\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Since the reference repeats every cycle, $\vect{e}(t_0)=\Pi^{\mathsf T}\vect{e}_k$ and $\Pi\vect{e}(t_N)=\vect{e}_{k+1}$. With $\delta\vect{t}=\ddwell_k$, \eqref{eq:augmented-error-linearization} becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}={}&\Phi_x\vect{e}_k+\Gamma_\tau\dtau_k\\
    &+\mathcal O\bigl((\|\vect{e}_k\|+\|\dtau_k\|)^2\bigr),\\
    \Phi_x={}&\Pi\Phi\Pi^{\mathsf T},\\
    \Gamma_\tau={}&\Pi\Gamma D.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-model}
\end{equation}
Here $\Phi_x\in\R^{n\times n}$ propagates the initial error, and $\Gamma_\tau\in\R^{n\times(N-1)}$ gives the effect of the switching offsets. Below, $\vect{e}_k$ and $\vect{e}$ denote the physical error in $\R^n$. The remainder uses fixed state and timing units.

Each column of $\Gamma_\tau$ describes how a small shift in one switching instant changes the state at the end of the cycle, as studied by \cite{patino2010} and \cite{flieller2006limitCycles}.

\subsection{Raw proportional timing feedback}

The controller converts the error at the start of each cycle into switching offsets using a fixed gain $K\in\R^{(N-1)\times n}$. This gain is chosen offline, for example by discrete LQR, and gives the raw proportional action
\begin{equation}
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k.
    \label{eq:raw-law}
\end{equation}
The raw law can request offsets that violate the dwell constraints. For example, the request in \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(c) places the last interior switching instant beyond $T$, although the final interval requires $t_{k,N-1}\le T-d_{\min}$. The following section conditions the offsets to satisfy these constraints.

\section{Solver-free dwell-time conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

The conditioner enforces the dwell constraints by multiplying every raw offset by the same factor $\beta_k\in[0,1]$. A value of 1 leaves the request unchanged, while smaller values move the switching instants toward the nominal schedule.

\subsection{Computing the conditioning factor}

The conditioner assumes a nominal schedule with strictly positive margins above the minimum dwell duration,
\begin{equation}
    \vect{m}=\bar{\vect{d}}-d_{\min}\vect{1}>\vect{0}.
    \label{eq:strict-margin}
\end{equation}
The dwell changes requested by the raw offsets are
\[
    \vect{q}_k=D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}.
\]
An interval with $q_{k,i}\ge0$ stays unchanged or becomes longer, so it imposes no additional restriction on the factor. For $q_{k,i}<0$, the interval becomes shorter and remains feasible when $\beta(-q_{k,i})\le m_i$. The smallest of these bounds, capped at one, gives the largest common factor:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \beta_k&=\min\left\{1,\ \min_{i:q_{k,i}<0}\frac{m_i}{-q_{k,i}}\right\},\\
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}&=\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioner}
\end{equation}
A single scan through the intervals evaluates this factor without an optimization solver:
\begin{quote}
\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{1em}\=\hspace{1em}\=\kill
$\beta_k\gets1$ \\
\textbf{for} $i=1,\ldots,N$ \textbf{do} \\
\>\textbf{if} $q_{k,i}<0$ \textbf{then} \\
\>\>$\mathit{limit}\gets m_i/(-q_{k,i})$ \\
\>\>$\beta_k\gets\min\{\beta_k,\mathit{limit}\}$ \\
\>\textbf{end if} \\
\textbf{end for} \\
$\dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}\gets\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}$
\end{tabbing}
\end{quote}
A feasible raw request is applied unchanged, with $\beta_k=1$. Otherwise, all offsets are scaled by the largest factor that keeps every dwell at or above $d_{\min}$, with at least one dwell reaching this limit. If no interval is shortened, then the formula returns $\beta_k=1$.

\subsection{Feasibility and maximality}

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility and maximality]
\label{prop:conditioner}
Under \eqref{eq:strict-margin}, the factor in \eqref{eq:conditioner} satisfies
\[
    \bar{\vect{d}}+\beta_k D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}.
\]
It is the largest factor in $[0,1]$ that satisfies all dwell constraints. For a finite raw action, $\beta_k>0$. The conditioner preserves the direction of a nonzero raw action, the mode order, and the cycle period. The factor can be computed in $O(N)$ time without an online solver.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The factor in \eqref{eq:conditioner} is no larger than any of the dwell bounds, so every applied interval lasts at least $d_{\min}$. A larger factor would either exceed one or make at least one interval too short.

For a finite raw request, the positive margins make every ratio used in the minimum positive, so $\beta_k>0$. Multiplying all offsets by this positive factor preserves the direction of a nonzero raw action. Since $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$, the changes in interval duration sum to zero and leave the period $T$ unchanged. Every applied duration is at least $d_{\min}>0$, so the switching instants keep their original order.

Computing the adjacent differences and scanning the interval bounds each take $O(N)$ operations.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Online execution and admissible region}

At the start of each cycle, the controller uses the state error to compute the raw offsets and then the common conditioning factor. The resulting schedule is applied during that cycle, with no computation delay included in the model.

The Raw-Action Admissible Region contains the errors for which the raw request already satisfies the dwell constraints. Substituting $\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k$ into those constraints gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C=\{\vect{e}:DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}.
    \label{eq:admissible-region}
\end{equation}
The scan returns $\beta_k=1$ inside $\mathcal C$ and $\beta_k<1$ outside, so it determines whether a reduction is needed without a separate membership test. \Cref{app:invariant-region} examines smaller fixed factors and their linearized invariance.

\section{Lyapunov stability and convex optimization}
\label{sec:stability}

The conditioner enforces the dwell constraints, while convergence requires a separate test. This test searches offline for one quadratic Lyapunov function that decreases for every conditioning factor in the linearized model.

\subsection{Conditioned matrix family}

The stability analysis uses the physical error $\vect{e}_k$ at the start of each cycle. The nominal schedule and the full raw feedback give the two endpoint matrices
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    A_0&=\Phi_x,\\
    A_1&=A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\Phi_x-\Gamma_\tau K.
\end{aligned}
    \label{eq:endpoints}
\end{equation}
The conditioner applies a fraction $\beta_k$ of the raw action, so substitution into the linearized model gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}&=A(\beta_k)\vect{e}_k,\\
    A(\beta)&=(1-\beta)A_0+\beta A_1
    =\Phi_x-\beta\Gamma_\tau K.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioned-family}
\end{equation}
This segment joins the nominal schedule at $\beta=0$ to the full raw action at $\beta=1$. It describes the linearized model, with the remainder in \eqref{eq:cycle-model} omitted. Finite raw requests give $\beta_k>0$, but the analysis also includes the limiting case $\beta=0$.

Stability of the endpoint matrices alone does not ensure that every matrix on the segment is stable. Even if each matrix is stable for a fixed $\beta$, changing $\beta$ between cycles can still cause instability. A common quadratic Lyapunov function provides a test that covers both cases.

\subsection{Common quadratic Lyapunov certificate}

One quadratic Lyapunov function can establish stability for a family of discrete-time systems \cite{mason2004commonQuadratic}. Hu et al. use this approach for discrete-time actuator saturation, checking decrease at the vertices of a convex family to certify contractive ellipsoids \cite{hu2002actuatorSaturation}. Here, the conditioning factor produces a segment of linearized cycle matrices, so the test uses its two endpoints.

The candidate Lyapunov function is
\[
    V(\vect{e})=\vect{e}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{e},\qquad P\succ0.
\]
The endpoint inequalities below require a decrease of at least $\eta\|\vect{e}\|_2^2$ in one cycle. Both endpoints must satisfy these inequalities with the same matrix $P$ and the same positive margin $\eta$.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.96\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/common_p_geometry.tex}}
    \caption{Common-$P$ schematic in error coordinates. Both endpoint images lie inside the unit level set of $V$. Every intermediate image $A(\beta)\vect{e}$ lies on the segment joining them.}
    \label{fig:common-p}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform stability under conditioning]
\label{thm:common-p}
Suppose there exist $P=P^{\mathsf T}\succ0$ and $\eta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}
    P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i\succeq\eta I_n,\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:endpoint-lmis}
\end{equation}
Then $V(\vect{e})=\vect{e}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{e}$ decreases for every nonzero state of \eqref{eq:conditioned-family} and every $\beta\in[0,1]$. The conditioned linearized model is uniformly exponentially stable for every sequence $\{\beta_k\}\subset[0,1]$, including the state-dependent factors in \eqref{eq:conditioner}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a fixed $P\succ0$, the Lyapunov decrease condition can be expressed through the block matrix
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal S(\beta)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}\\
        A(\beta)&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:schur-block}
\end{equation}
The block $-P^{-1}$ is negative definite, and its Schur complement is $-P+A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)$. Hence
\[
    \mathcal S(\beta)\prec0
    \Longleftrightarrow
    A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)-P\prec0.
\]
The inequalities in \eqref{eq:endpoint-lmis} therefore make both $\mathcal S(0)$ and $\mathcal S(1)$ negative definite.

Because the same $P$ is used throughout the interval, the diagonal blocks stay fixed. The relation $A(\beta)=(1-\beta)A_0+\beta A_1$ then gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal S(\beta)
    &=(1-\beta)\begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A_0^{\mathsf T}\\
        A_0&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}\\
    &\quad+\beta\begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A_1^{\mathsf T}\\
        A_1&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}\prec0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:schur-convex-combination}
\end{equation}
Both endpoint blocks are negative definite, and their weights are nonnegative and sum to one. Their convex combination is therefore negative definite for every $\beta\in[0,1]$.

The Schur complement then gives a strict decrease in $V$ for every nonzero error and every $\beta\in[0,1]$. Since the matrix family varies continuously with $\beta$ over this compact interval, the decrease has a uniform positive margin. The same quadratic function therefore establishes uniform exponential stability for any sequence of conditioning factors.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:common-p} shows both endpoint images and their joining segment inside one Lyapunov ellipsoid. The certificate covers the full factor interval and all errors of the linearized model, including those outside $\mathcal C$. It does not establish that the approximation is valid at arbitrary physical errors or give a region of attraction for the nonlinear plant.

\subsection{Offline semidefinite program}

Related formulations use a common Lyapunov matrix and inequalities at the vertices of a matrix family. They appear in discrete-time robust stability \cite{deOliveira1999lmiDiscrete} and in robust control of PWM converters described by polytopic, continuous-time averaged models \cite{olalla2009robustLqrPwm}.

After the gain $K$ is chosen, the endpoint matrices $A_0=\Phi_x$ and $A_1=A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ are fixed. The Lyapunov matrix $P$ and the margin $\eta$ are then computed offline by the semidefinite program
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \underset{P=P^{\mathsf T},\,\eta}{\operatorname{maximize}}\quad&\eta\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
    &\operatorname{tr}(P)=n,\\
    &P\succeq\varepsilon_P I_n,\\
    &P-A_0^{\mathsf T}PA_0\succeq\eta I_n,\\
    &P-A_1^{\mathsf T}PA_1\succeq\eta I_n,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:common-p-sdp}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_P=10^{-7}$ sets a positive lower bound on the eigenvalues of $P$. The trace constraint fixes the scale of $P$, which would otherwise be arbitrary.

For fixed endpoints, \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} is affine in $P$ and $\eta$ and tests the chosen gain rather than changing it. The proof's block matrix is affine in $\beta$ when $P$ is fixed, but contains $P^{-1}$ and is therefore nonlinear in $P$. The numerical solve uses \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} in scaled coordinates, avoiding the matrix inverse.

We verify the certificate directly, since successful solver termination alone does not establish it. The returned $P$ is first symmetrized, then used to recompute the endpoint decrease matrices and their smallest eigenvalues:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q_i&=P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i,\qquad i\in\{0,1\},\\
    \mu&=\min\{\lambda_{\min}(Q_0),\lambda_{\min}(Q_1)\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:verified-margin}
\end{equation}
The certificate is accepted only when $P$ is positive definite, its trace has the required value, and $\mu>0$. This positive margin verifies the common decrease required by \cref{thm:common-p}.

This is a sufficient test, so failure to find a positive common margin leaves stability unresolved. Including $\beta=0$ requires $A_0$ to be Schur, which excludes an unstable nominal endpoint even if a restricted range of conditioning factors could stabilize the system.

The stability guarantee applies to the linearized model with conditioning. Simulations show how the exact nonlinear system responds to large errors, including the first cycles when the raw offsets are reduced.

\section{Application to a three-cell converter}
\label{sec:case-study}

The study tests faster convergence and dwell feasibility on a converter that is stable without feedback. MPC-5 is a model predictive controller that optimizes the switching-time offsets over a prediction horizon of $N_p=5$ switching cycles. It provides a limited performance and computation reference.

\subsection{Benchmark and controller parameters}

We use the three-cell multilevel DC--DC converter benchmark of Patino et al. \cite{patino2010}. The state $\vect{x}=[v_{C_1},v_{C_2},i_L]^{\mathsf T}$ contains the two capacitor voltages and the inductor current. For binary switch commands $u_1,u_2,u_3\in\{0,1\}$, the plant matrices are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&(u_2-u_1)/C_1\\
0&0&(u_3-u_2)/C_2\\
(u_1-u_2)/L&(u_2-u_3)/L&-R/L
\end{bmatrix},\\
\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\Eu_3/L\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:converter}
\end{equation}
Both $A_{\vect{u}}$ and $\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}$ depend on the switch state. The fixed mode sequence is $(0,1,3,7,2,0,4,7,4)$, with each mode identified by $4u_1+2u_2+u_3$.

The published switching times $(0,66,88,110,132,154,220,242,264,286)$~\si{\micro\second} do not return the state exactly to the reported anchor $\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=(9.9247,19.2928,0.9823)^{\mathsf T}$ after one cycle. A single least-squares adjustment preserves this anchor, the \SI{\NominalPeriodMicroseconds}{\micro\second} period, and the \SI{\BenchmarkDwellBound}{\micro\second} minimum dwell used in the nominal design, giving
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}

    &218.210,240.380,263.828,286)~\si{\micro\second}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:benchmark-schedule}
\end{equation}
At full precision, the schedule has closure error \NominalClosureError{} in the infinity norm and maximum timing correction \MaximumBoundaryCorrection{}~\si{\micro\second}. This reconciles the published data rather than contributing to reference trajectory design.

\subsection{Numerical scaling and feedback gains}

The numerical scales in \cref{tab:parameters} give
\begin{equation}
    \vect{z}=S_x^{-1}\vect{e},\qquad
    \widehat A_i=S_x^{-1}A_iS_x,\quad i\in\{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:normalized-endpoints}
\end{equation}
This numerical scaling preserves the endpoint eigenvalues and is optional for the stability proof.

With $\vect{v}=\dtau/t_s$, the normalized input matrix is $B_n=S_x^{-1}\Gamma_\tau t_s$. Discrete LQR on $(\widehat A_0,B_n)$ gives $\vect{v}^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K_n\vect{z}$, corresponding to $K=t_sK_nS_x^{-1}$ in physical coordinates.

The aggressive tuning uses conditioning, while the conservative tuning satisfies the dwell constraints directly in this experiment. The bound for nominal design in \cref{tab:parameters} is larger than the applied bound, which is assumed rather than calibrated on hardware.

Finite-difference checks agree with the derived state and timing sensitivities. The measured prediction residual shows the quadratic decay expected from the linearization.

\subsection{Numerical Lyapunov certificate}

For the aggressive gain, Julia solves \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} with normalized endpoints $\widehat A_0$ and $\widehat A_1$ and trace constraint $\operatorname{tr}(P_n)=3$, using Convex.jl and Clarabel. It returns Lyapunov matrix $P_n$ and optimal status with solver margin $\eta_n=\num{\LyapunovEta}$. To six decimal places, the matrix is
\begin{equation}
    P_n\approx\LyapunovMatrix.
    \label{eq:numerical-p}
\end{equation}
The normalized endpoint decrease matrices provide a direct check beyond solver status:
\[
    Q_{i,n}=P_n-\widehat A_i^{\mathsf T}P_n\widehat A_i,\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Using full precision, their smallest eigenvalues and that of $P_n$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{\min}(P_n)&=\num{\LyapunovMinEigenvalue},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_{0,n})&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointZeroDecrease},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_{1,n})&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointOneDecrease}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:numerical-margins}
\end{equation}
Both endpoint margins are positive, with minimum $\mu_n=\num{\LyapunovDecrease}$. These margins and the reported trace refer to normalized coordinates.

The certificate is expressed in physical coordinates through
\[
\begin{aligned}
    P&=S_x^{-\mathsf T}P_nS_x^{-1},\\
    P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i&=S_x^{-\mathsf T}Q_{i,n}S_x^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
These transformations preserve positive definiteness, so the checks provide the certificate in physical coordinates required by \cref{thm:common-p}. The feedback gain and plant trajectories remain unchanged. The matrix is checked both before and after rounding for display, using floating-point rather than interval arithmetic.

\subsection{Host computational cost}
\label{sec:computational-cost}

The matched MATLAB measurements in \cref{tab:results} give median execution times of \SI{\ProposedMatchedMedianUs}{\micro\second} for the proposed controller and \SI{\MpcMatchedMedianUs}{\micro\second} for MPC-5. Their p95 times are \SI{\ProposedMatchedPninetyfiveUs}{\micro\second} and \SI{\MpcMatchedPninetyfiveUs}{\micro\second}, respectively. Both controllers run on the same 64-bit Windows desktop using MATLAB R\MatchedTimingMatlabRelease{}. The recorded processor is an AMD Family 25 Model 97 with 16 logical processors, and MATLAB allows up to 8 computation threads.

Both controllers process the same \MatchedTimingStates{} physical states from the first 12 cycle starts of each response. We interleave \MatchedTimingSamples{} timed calls per controller after \MatchedTimingWarmup{} warm-up calls each. Timing covers physical error calculation, required normalization, control, schedule conversion, and feasibility checks, excluding offline design, plant propagation, and acquisition. First calls are recorded separately, and every MPC solve starts from a zero plan without reuse.

A separate microbenchmark isolates conditioning from the rest of the controller. It uses \num{\BetaTimingIndividualSamples} individual calls on an \BetaTimingCpu{}. MATLAB p95 is \SI{\BetaTimingMatlabBetaPninetyfiveUs}{\micro\second} for $\beta$ alone and \SI{\BetaTimingMatlabFullPninetyfiveUs}{\micro\second} for full conditioning.

For double-precision C++ compiled with GCC \BetaTimingGccVersion{} and \texttt{-O3}, the individual-call p95 reaches the timer floor of \SI{\BetaTimingCppTimerFloorUs}{\micro\second}. For batches of \BetaTimingBatchSize{} calls, the p95 of the mean time per call is \SI{\BetaTimingCppBetaBatchPninetyfiveNs}{\nano\second} for $\beta$ and \SI{\BetaTimingCppFullBatchPninetyfiveNs}{\nano\second} for full conditioning. These percentiles describe batch averages, not individual latencies.

The p95 is the observed 95th percentile, not a worst-case bound. The C++ results concern conditioning alone, not a comparison with MATLAB MPC. None of these desktop measurements guarantees embedded deadlines.

\section{Discussion}

MPC can include constraints directly in its optimization problem. Here, MPC-5 lowers the evaluation cost and crosses the threshold earlier, but takes longer in the matched MATLAB measurements. This trade-off matters when online optimization cannot meet the deadlines of fast control loops. The benchmark does not establish a general performance ranking.

The proposed LQR-based controller instead applies the largest dwell-feasible scale for any finite request using a fixed-gain multiplication, adjacent differences, and a minimum scan. Feasibility follows from this scaling, whereas the offline common-$P$ test certifies stability of the conditioned linearized family.

The sensitivity builds on \cite{patino2010} and recovers the model with a common state matrix in \cite{marcolino2021}. Direction-preserving scaling also appears in control allocation, as discussed by \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. The contribution is the closed-form treatment of coupled dwell inequalities and its connection to stability certification, not new LQR synthesis or a general Lyapunov theorem.

The common-$P$ certificate is sufficient rather than necessary and can fail for otherwise stable controllers. It requires a common matrix and a Schur zero-feedback endpoint, so this study concerns accelerated convergence of an already stable open loop. The nonlinear trajectories do not certify a region of attraction.

Hardware calibration of the \SI{\AppliedScheduleDwellBound}{\micro\second} bound, quantization, delays, dead time, estimation, uncertainty, and experimental validation remain outside scope. The desktop timings do not establish embedded feasibility.

\section{Conclusion}

The proposed controller is a candidate for applications where the computational cost of MPC is a limiting factor. It combines LQR feedback with closed-form dwell conditioning to enforce the dwell constraints without online optimization. In the converter benchmark, it achieves shorter measured execution times than MPC-5 at some loss of performance. Its offline certificate guarantees stability of the conditioned linearized model. Certification of the full nonlinear transient and validation on target hardware remain necessary before deployment in critical systems.

\section{Feasible-region expansion under timing-action scaling}
\label{app:invariant-region}

\subsection{Expansion of the dwell-feasible region}

A smaller fixed conditioning factor allows larger state errors while satisfying the dwell constraints. With $\beta\in(0,1]$ fixed, the timing action is $\dtau=-\beta K\vect{e}$, so the feasible region is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal F_\beta&=\{\vect{e}:\beta DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}
    =\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal C,\\
    \mathcal F_1&=\mathcal C.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fixed-beta-feasible}
\end{equation}
The raw-action region $\mathcal C$ expands by $1/\beta$, giving $\mathcal F_{\beta_b}\subseteq\mathcal F_{\beta_a}$ whenever $0<\beta_a<\beta_b\le1$. In the three-state converter, volumes scale as $\beta^{-3}$, so factors $\LowerComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, $\MiddleComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, and $1$ give volume ratios $\LowerComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, $\MiddleComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, and $1$. This expansion concerns current-cycle feasibility, not stability over successive cycles.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{appendix_raw_action_region.pdf}
    \caption{Dwell-feasible regions for fixed factors $\beta=\LowerComparisonConditioningFactor{},\MiddleComparisonConditioningFactor{},1$, with early cycle-start errors from the conditioned simulation. (a) Three-dimensional view. (b)--(d) Coordinate-plane projections. Smaller factors admit larger errors.}
    \label{fig:appendix-region}
\end{figure*}

In \cref{fig:appendix-region}, lines connect cycle-start errors from the simulation with state-dependent conditioning. The initial error lies inside $\mathcal F_{\LowerComparisonConditioningFactor{}}$ and outside $\mathcal F_{\MiddleComparisonConditioningFactor{}}$, consistent with $\beta_0\approx0.294$. The raw action becomes feasible at cycle \FirstInvariantRegionCycle{}. The planar views are projections rather than slices, so membership is determined from the full three-dimensional inequalities.

\subsection{Feasibility over successive cycles}

With the factor fixed, continued dwell feasibility requires the error to remain in $\mathcal F_\beta$. The linearized dynamics are
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=M_\beta\vect{e}_k,\qquad
    M_\beta=\Phi_x-\beta\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
Offline calculations with MPT3 \citep{herceg2013mpt3} and preimage containment \citep{gilbert1991maximal} verify positive invariance for all three plotted factors:
\begin{equation}
    M_\beta\mathcal F_\beta\subseteq\mathcal F_\beta.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-invariance}
\end{equation}
Each vertex $\vect{v}$ of $\mathcal F_\beta$ satisfies $\beta DK M_\beta\vect{v}\le\vect{m}$ within numerical tolerance. Every point of this bounded, convex polyhedron is a convex combination of vertices, so linearity extends the check to the whole region. Invariance therefore preserves dwell feasibility over subsequent cycles, while \cref{thm:common-p} supplies convergence because $M_\beta=A(\beta)$.

These linearized checks with fixed factors establish neither nonlinear invariance nor an enlarged nonlinear region of attraction for state-dependent conditioning. The common-$P$ theorem holds independently of these feasibility checks.

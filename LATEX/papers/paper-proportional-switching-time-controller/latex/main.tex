\title[Dwell-conditioned switching-time feedback]{Dwell-Conditioned Cycle-to-Cycle Switching-Time Feedback for Mode-Dependent Switched-Affine Systems}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Daniel} \sur{Vieira}}\email{danielvie@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Roberto Kawakami Harrop} \sur{Galv\~ao}}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Systems and Control}, \orgname{Instituto Tecnol\'ogico de Aeron\'autica}, \orgaddress{\city{S\~ao Jos\'e dos Campos}, \state{S\~ao Paulo}, \country{Brazil}}}

\abstract{Static cycle-to-cycle feedback regulates a reference periodic trajectory by shifting the interior switching instants of a fixed-period mode sequence. Its raw timing action can nevertheless produce inadmissibly short or negative dwell durations. We derive the one-cycle model for mode-dependent switched-affine dynamics and condition the complete raw action by its largest dwell-feasible scalar factor. The conditioner preserves the action direction, mode order, and cycle period and requires an $O(N)$ scan without an online solver. Uniform scaling places the conditioned linearized matrices on a segment between the nominal cycle matrix and the raw closed-loop matrix. An offline semidefinite program seeks a common quadratic Lyapunov function at these two endpoints. We prove that strict endpoint decrease implies uniform exponential stability for every sequence of conditioning factors in $[0,1]$. A three-cell DC--DC converter benchmark admits this certificate. Exact switched-affine simulations compare the controlled and open-loop three-dimensional trajectories and show eventual open-loop convergence to the same reference trajectory. Conditioned feedback reaches a normalized error below $0.01$ at cycle \ConditionedSettlingCycle{}, compared with persistent open-loop crossing at cycle \OpenLoopConvergenceCycle{}, while enforcing the assumed \AppliedScheduleDwellBound{}~\si{\micro\second} dwell bound. The common Lyapunov result concerns the linearized family; exact nonlinear stability remains local, and the large-error response is simulation evidence.}

\keywords{switched-affine systems, periodic trajectories, switching-time control, dwell-time conditioning, common quadratic Lyapunov function, semidefinite programming}

\section{Introduction}

Power converters control voltage and current by moving through a sequence of switch configurations. In practice, the switching pattern must respect electrical limits, switching frequency, a minimum dwell duration for each configuration, and a short computation time \cite{karamanakos2020mpcPowerElectronics}. Keeping the individual configurations explicit gives a hybrid-affine or switched-affine model \cite{albea2021hybridAffinePwm}. Repeating the pattern produces a periodic trajectory rather than a constant state, and controlling such trajectories is an established problem for switched-affine systems \cite{egidio2020,xu2024fcsLimitCycle}. We consider regulation at a selected cycle anchor. At each cycle start, the controller steers the state toward that point on the reference periodic trajectory.

Predictive control tackles the problem by solving a constrained optimization problem online. Recent work reduces the online burden through Laguerre parameterization, dedicated fixed-frequency solvers, and candidate pruning \cite{saeed2022laguerre,yang2022fixed,augustine2023mpc}. Controllers also differ in what they may change. State-dependent switching laws choose the active subsystem \cite{egidio2022rank}, where switching-time control keeps an ordered sequence and moves its transition instants. We take this same approach, changing the transition instants and keepping the rest of the parameters the same. At each cycle start, the error is computed and the offsets applied during that cycle, with no online solver.

Patino et al. studied mode-dependent cyclic systems and how each switching instant affects the trajectory. They applied an online predictive controller to adjust the switching instants and track an optimal periodic trajectory \cite{patino2010}. Marcolino et al. considered a fixed-period cycle controlled by changing its interior switching instants. They used a discrete LQR and dwell-constrained predictive control to apply corrections based on the one-cycle model \cite{marcolino2021}. Their model has a common state matrix with switched actuator levels. Each interior switching instant separates two dwell durations, corresponding to the modes active before and after it. Delaying the instant lengthens the preceding dwell and shortens the following one, so the dwell limits are coupled. The predictive controller enforces these limits through online optimization, and its feasible set is characterized through polyhedral projection \cite{marcolino2021}. The standalone LQR comparison instead clips the offsets one at a time, which changes the requested direction and may shift the final scheduled instant \cite{marcolino2021}. Projecting a raw action directly onto the feasible set would require a quadratic program and would generally change its direction. Direction-preserving control allocation scales the complete command, but it also uses online optimization and does not address fixed-period dwell constraints \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. The remaining problem is to find the largest feasible scale directly, preserving the timing-action direction, mode order, and cycle period without an online solver.

We adapt direction-preserving scaling to fixed-period switching schedules. Each dwell constraint gives an upper bound on the scale, and the smallest bound is the largest feasible factor in $[0,1]$. Checking all $N$ bounds takes $O(N)$ time and requires no online solver. Scaling the complete raw offset vector preserves its direction, the mode order, and the cycle period. For the linearized raw closed loop, an offline polyhedral iteration finds the maximal invariant subset of the Raw-Action Admissible Region. Outside this set, the conditioner guarantees current-cycle dwell feasibility but not convergence; the nonlinear guarantee remains local. \Cref{sec:model} derives the cycle model, \cref{sec:conditioning} develops the conditioner and invariant region, and \cref{sec:case-study} presents the converter study.

\section{Problem formulation and one-cycle model}
\label{sec:model}

The control objective is to approach a prescribed periodic trajectory without changing its mode sequence or cycle period. We first express this objective at cycle starts, then derive how shifts of the interior switching instants change the next cycle-start state.

\subsection{Plant, reference trajectory, and timing coordinates}

Consider the switched-affine plant
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vect{x}}(t)=A_{\sigma(t)}\vect{x}(t)+\vect{b}_{\sigma(t)},
    \qquad \vect{x}(t)\in\R^n.
    \label{eq:plant}
\end{equation}
Every cycle visits the fixed sequence $(q_1,\ldots,q_N)$. Its nominal cycle-relative boundaries and dwell durations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    0&=\bar t_0<\bar t_1<\cdots<\bar t_N=T,\\
    \bar d_i&=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Repeating this schedule generates the reference periodic trajectory $\bar{\vect{x}}(t)$. Its cycle anchor is $\vect{x}^{\star}=\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=\bar{\vect{x}}(T)$. With $\vect{x}_k=\vect{x}(kT)$, the cycle-start error is $\vect{e}_k=\vect{x}_k-\vect{x}^{\star}$. The target is this phase-aligned trajectory, not a constant average operating point.

The controller moves only the $N-1$ interior instants:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    t_{k,i}&=\bar t_i+\delta\tau_{k,i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\\
    \delta\tau_{k,0}&=\delta\tau_{k,N}=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
During interval $i$ of cycle $k$, the plant uses mode $q_i$ between the absolute times $kT+t_{k,i-1}$ and $kT+t_{k,i}$. The applied dwell vector satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{d}_k&=\bar{\vect{d}}+\ddwell_k,\qquad \ddwell_k=D\dtau_k,\\
    D_{ij}&=\begin{cases}
        1,&i=j,\\
        -1,&i=j+1,\\
        0,&\text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:incidence-map}
\end{equation}
where $D\in\R^{N\times(N-1)}$. Thus delaying one instant lengthens the preceding interval and shortens the following one by the same amount. Since $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$, every offset vector preserves $T$. The remaining requirement is $\vect{d}_k\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}$ for a specified $d_{\min}>0$.

\Cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(a)--(b) illustrates the timing coordinates using the converter schedule introduced in \cref{sec:case-study}. In panel (b), delaying instant 8 by \SI{12}{\micro\second} lengthens dwell 8 and shortens dwell 9 by the same amount, with all other offsets zero. The shortened dwell remains above the assumed \AppliedScheduleDwellBound{}~\si{\micro\second} bound. This isolated offset illustrates the dwell coupling; panel (c) instead shows the aggressive controller's first-cycle raw request.

\subsection{Exact one-cycle map}

Introduce the augmented state, interval generator, and nominal transition matrix
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \chi&=\begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    F_i=\begin{bmatrix}A_{q_i}&\vect{b}_{q_i}\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&0\end{bmatrix},\\
    E_i&=e^{F_i\bar d_i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:augmented-generator}
\end{equation}
On interval $i$, $\dot\chi=F_i\chi$, so the exact cycle propagation is
\begin{equation}
    \chi_{k+1}=e^{F_Nd_{k,N}}\cdots e^{F_1d_{k,1}}\chi_k.
    \label{eq:exact-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
The rightmost factor acts first; matrices belonging to different modes cannot generally be interchanged. Define
\begin{equation}
    \Pi=\begin{bmatrix}I_n&\vect{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \iota=\begin{bmatrix}I_n\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The physical one-cycle map is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal P(\vect{x},\dtau)={}&\Pi e^{F_N[\bar d_N+(D\dtau)_N]}\cdots\\
    &\quad e^{F_1[\bar d_1+(D\dtau)_1]}
    \begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:exact-cycle-map}
\end{equation}
The reference schedule and anchor satisfy $\mathcal P(\vect{x}^{\star},\vect{0})=\vect{x}^{\star}$. They are given design data, not optimization variables of the timing controller.

\subsection{Linearization in the independent switching instants}

For a dwell change $\delta d_i$, the single-interval expansion is
\begin{equation}
    e^{F_i(\bar d_i+\delta d_i)}=E_i+F_iE_i\delta d_i
    +\mathcal O(\delta d_i^2).
    \label{eq:interval-first-order}
\end{equation}
Expanding the ordered product, while retaining only terms containing one dwell change, gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    e^{F_N(\bar d_N+\delta d_N)}\cdots e^{F_1(\bar d_1+\delta d_1)}\\
    =\widetilde\Phi+\sum_{i=1}^N J_i\delta d_i+\mathcal O(\|\ddwell\|^2),\\
    \widetilde\Phi=E_N\cdots E_1,\\
    J_i=E_N\cdots E_{i+1}F_iE_i\cdots E_1.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:ordered-product-first-order}
\end{equation}
An empty product is the identity. In particular, $J_1=E_N\cdots E_2F_1E_1$ and $J_N=F_NE_N\cdots E_1$. Write $\chi_k=\chi^{\star}+\iota\vect{e}_k$, with $\chi^{\star}=[(\vect{x}^{\star})^{\mathsf T}\ 1]^{\mathsf T}$. Since $\widetilde\Phi\chi^{\star}=\chi^{\star}$, subtracting the nominal propagation cancels the zeroth-order term. The products $\delta d_i\vect{e}_k$ and $\delta d_i\delta d_j$ are second order, leaving
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \chi_{k+1}-\chi^{\star}={}&\widetilde\Phi\iota\vect{e}_k
    +\sum_{i=1}^N J_i\chi^{\star}\delta d_{k,i}\\
    &+\mathcal O\bigl((\|\vect{e}_k\|+\|\ddwell_k\|)^2\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Projecting onto the physical state and using $\ddwell_k=D\dtau_k$ yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}&=\Phi\vect{e}_k+\Gamma_\tau\dtau_k
    +\mathcal O\bigl((\|\vect{e}_k\|+\|\dtau_k\|)^2\bigr),\\
    \Phi&=\Pi\widetilde\Phi\iota
    =e^{A_{q_N}\bar d_N}\cdots e^{A_{q_1}\bar d_1},\\
    G_d&=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi J_1\chi^{\star}&\cdots&\Pi J_N\chi^{\star}\end{bmatrix},\\
    \Gamma_\tau&=G_dD.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-model}
\end{equation}
The remainder is understood in fixed state and timing units. The augmented product has the block form
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi&\vect{h}\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&1\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:augmented-cycle-block-form}
\end{equation}
Its unit eigenvalue belongs to the auxiliary constant coordinate. Stability is tested on the physical error dynamics, not on this augmented matrix.

Each column of $D$ subtracts two adjacent dwell sensitivities. Factoring them gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}={}&e^{A_{q_N}\bar d_N}\cdots e^{A_{q_{i+1}}\bar d_{i+1}}\\
    &\times\bigl[(A_{q_i}-A_{q_{i+1}})\bar{\vect{x}}(\bar t_i)
    +\vect{b}_{q_i}-\vect{b}_{q_{i+1}}\bigr].
\end{split}
\label{eq:timing-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The bracket is the change in the affine vector field caused by trading time between adjacent modes. The remaining transitions carry this effect to the cycle end. This recovers the switching-sensitivity structure in \cite{patino2010}; switching-time sensitivities also appear in the hybrid limit-cycle analysis of \cite{flieller2006limitCycles}.

For the special case $A_{q_i}=A_c$ and $\vect{b}_{q_i}=B_c\vect{u}_{i-1}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi=e^{A_cT},\quad
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}=e^{A_c(T-\bar t_i)}B_c(\vect{u}_{i-1}-\vect{u}_i),
    \label{eq:marcolino-specialization}
\end{equation}
which is the fixed-period switched-actuator model of \cite{marcolino2021}, with the corresponding input-jump sign convention. Mode-dependent state matrices require the full expression in \eqref{eq:timing-jacobian}.

\subsection{Raw proportional timing feedback}

A fixed gain $K\in\R^{(N-1)\times n}$ requests
\begin{equation}
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k,\qquad
    A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\Phi-\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:raw-law}
\end{equation}
The gain can be designed from \eqref{eq:cycle-model}, for example by discrete LQR. Here the gain is fixed before conditioning and certification. Although a Schur $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ gives a locally stabilizing raw law, it does not make every requested schedule admissible. In \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(c), the raw request moves the last interior instant beyond $T$, making $d_N=T-t_{k,N-1}<0$. A minimum dwell constraint is stricter still: it requires $t_{k,N-1}\le T-d_{\min}$ even before the boundaries cross. The next section enforces all such adjacent-dwell constraints; \cref{sec:stability} examines the resulting conditioned dynamics.

\section{Solver-free dwell-time conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

Minimum dwell durations couple adjacent offsets through $D$. Independent clipping need not preserve the direction of the feedback action. Instead, we apply one scalar factor to the complete raw vector.

Assume that every nominal interval has strict margin,
\begin{equation}
    \vect{m}=\bar{\vect{d}}-d_{\min}\vect{1}>\vect{0}.
    \label{eq:strict-margin}
\end{equation}
The Raw-Action Admissible Region is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C=\{\vect{e}:DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}.
    \label{eq:admissible-region}
\end{equation}
Inside $\mathcal C$, the raw action already satisfies all dwell constraints. Outside it, at least one requested interval is too short. With $\vect{q}_k=D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}$, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \beta_k&=\min\left\{1,\ \min_{i:q_{k,i}<0}\frac{m_i}{-q_{k,i}}\right\},\\
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}&=\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioner}
\end{equation}
where a minimum over an empty set is $+\infty$. Only intervals that the raw action would shorten impose upper bounds on $\beta_k$.

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility and maximality]
\label{prop:conditioner}
Under \eqref{eq:strict-margin}, \eqref{eq:conditioner} returns the largest $\beta\in[0,1]$ for which every applied dwell satisfies $\bar{\vect{d}}+\beta D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}$. For a finite raw action, $\beta>0$. The conditioner preserves the direction of a nonzero raw action, mode order, and cycle period. Computing the factor takes $O(N)$ time without an online solver.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $q_{k,i}\ge0$, $m_i+\beta q_{k,i}>0$ for all $\beta\in[0,1]$. For $q_{k,i}<0$, feasibility is equivalent to $\beta\le m_i/(-q_{k,i})$. Intersecting these bounds with $[0,1]$ gives \eqref{eq:conditioner}, proving feasibility and maximality. Every finite bound is positive. Uniform positive scaling preserves direction, and $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$ preserves $T$. Since $d_{\min}>0$, the boundaries remain ordered. Adjacent differences compute $\vect{q}_k$, and one scan evaluates the $N$ bounds.
\end{proof}

At each cycle start, the controller therefore measures $\vect{x}_k$, computes the raw action, scans the dwell changes, and applies the scaled offsets. If $\beta_k<1$, at least one dwell reaches its minimum. No set-membership test or optimization is required online. The polyhedron $\mathcal C$ explains where conditioning is inactive. \Cref{app:invariant-region} compares the feasible regions for smaller fixed factors and checks their invariance under the corresponding linearized dynamics.

\section{Lyapunov stability and convex optimization}
\label{sec:stability}

Feasibility alone does not imply convergence. This section gives a sufficient stability condition for the complete conditioned linearized family, including cycles in which $\beta_k<1$.

\subsection{Conditioned matrix family}

Let $S_x$ be a fixed positive diagonal state-scale matrix and set $\vect{z}_k=S_x^{-1}\vect{e}_k$. Define the normalized endpoints
\begin{equation}
    A_0=S_x^{-1}\Phi S_x,\qquad
    A_1=S_x^{-1}A_{\mathrm{cl}}S_x.
    \label{eq:endpoints}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:conditioner} into the first-order model gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{z}_{k+1}&=A(\beta_k)\vect{z}_k,\\
    A(\beta)&=(1-\beta)A_0+\beta A_1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioned-family}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:conditioned-family} omits the nonlinear remainder in \eqref{eq:cycle-model}. Although the implemented conditioner returns $\beta>0$ for finite errors, certifying the closed interval $[0,1]$ includes its limiting zero-feedback endpoint. Similarity by $S_x$ changes neither endpoint's eigenvalues.

Schur stability of $A_0$ and $A_1$ separately is insufficient to establish stability of every matrix in the segment or of their time-varying products. We seek one quadratic function that decreases for both endpoints.

\subsection{Common quadratic Lyapunov certificate}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.96\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/common_p_geometry.tex}}
    \caption{Schematic common-$P$ geometry in normalized error coordinates. The ellipse is the unit level set of $V$; the segment joins the endpoint images $A_0\vect{z}$ and $A_1\vect{z}$, with an intermediate image $A(\beta)\vect{z}$ marked.}
    \label{fig:common-p}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform stability under conditioning]
\label{thm:common-p}
Suppose there exist $P=P^{\mathsf T}\succ0$ and $\eta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}
    P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i\succeq\eta I_n,\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:endpoint-lmis}
\end{equation}
Then $V(\vect{z})=\vect{z}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{z}$ decreases for every nonzero state of \eqref{eq:conditioned-family}, uniformly over $\beta\in[0,1]$. For any sequence $\{\beta_k\}\subset[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \|\vect{z}_k\|_2&\le
    \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(P)}{\lambda_{\min}(P)}}\,r^k\|\vect{z}_0\|_2,\\
    r&=\sqrt{1-\frac{\eta}{\lambda_{\max}(P)}}<1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:uniform-bound}
\end{equation}
In particular, the result holds for the state-dependent factor in \eqref{eq:conditioner}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the same $P\succ0$ and $\eta>0$ at both endpoints, define
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal S_\eta(\beta)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        P-\eta I_n&A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}P\\
        PA(\beta)&P
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:schur-block}
\end{equation}
Since $P\succ0$, the Schur complement of its lower-right block gives the equivalence
\[
    \mathcal S_\eta(\beta)\succeq0
    \Longleftrightarrow
    P-A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)\succeq\eta I_n.
\]
The endpoint conditions therefore give $\mathcal S_\eta(0),\mathcal S_\eta(1)\succeq0$. The affine dependence on $\beta$ yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal S_\eta(\beta)
    &=(1-\beta)\mathcal S_\eta(0)\\
    &\quad+\beta\mathcal S_\eta(1)\succeq0
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:schur-convex-combination}
\end{equation}
for every $\beta\in[0,1]$, by convexity of the positive semidefinite cone. The Schur complement then gives, for $\vect{z}_{k+1}=A(\beta_k)\vect{z}_k$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    V(\vect{z}_{k+1})-V(\vect{z}_k)
    &\le-\eta\|\vect{z}_k\|_2^2\\
    &\le-\frac{\eta}{\lambda_{\max}(P)}V(\vect{z}_k).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The same bound holds independently at every cycle, without any assumption on how $\beta_k$ is selected. Iteration and the inequalities $\lambda_{\min}(P)\|\vect{z}\|_2^2\le V(\vect{z})\le\lambda_{\max}(P)\|\vect{z}\|_2^2$ give \eqref{eq:uniform-bound}.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:common-p} illustrates why the same $P$ matters. For every $\vect{z}$ with $V(\vect{z})=1$, both endpoint images lie strictly inside the same ellipsoid. Their convex combination $A(\beta)\vect{z}$ lies on the segment joining them, which remains inside that convex sublevel set. Using one $V$ at every cycle makes the decrease comparable even when $\beta_k$ changes. The drawing is a two-dimensional schematic, not a converter trajectory or a nonlinear region of attraction.

The Schur-complement argument proves decrease on the entire matrix segment, not only at sampled values of $\beta$. It certifies the linearized conditioned model for all initial errors in that model, including those outside $\mathcal C$. It does not make the linear approximation valid at arbitrary physical errors.

\subsection{Offline semidefinite program}

For a fixed gain and fixed endpoint matrices, the certificate can be found by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \underset{P=P^{\mathsf T},\,\eta}{\operatorname{maximize}}\quad&\eta\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
    &\operatorname{tr}(P)=n,\\
    &P\succeq\varepsilon_P I_n,\\
    &P-A_0^{\mathsf T}PA_0\succeq\eta I_n,\\
    &P-A_1^{\mathsf T}PA_1\succeq\eta I_n,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:common-p-sdp}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_P=10^{-7}$. The trace constraint removes the arbitrary positive scale of $P$. For fixed $A_0,A_1$, both these inequalities and their equivalent inverse-free block form $\mathcal S_\eta(0),\mathcal S_\eta(1)\succeq0$ are affine in $P,\eta$. The Julia implementation uses the smaller inequalities in \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp}. The gain $K$ is fixed, not jointly optimized. A stability certificate requires a positive verified decrease margin, not merely successful solver termination.

After solving, we symmetrize the returned $P$ and recompute
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q_i&=P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i,\qquad i\in\{0,1\},\\
    \mu&=\min\{\lambda_{\min}(Q_0),\lambda_{\min}(Q_1)\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:verified-margin}
\end{equation}
Positive definiteness, trace normalization, and $\mu>0$ are checked directly. The verified margin $\mu$ can replace $\eta$ in \eqref{eq:uniform-bound}. Maximizing $\eta$ under trace normalization does not directly minimize the resulting rate bound, which also depends on $\lambda_{\max}(P)$.

The certificate is sufficient, not necessary. In particular, including $\beta=0$ requires $A_0$ to be Schur. An unstable open-loop endpoint cannot satisfy this full-interval test, even if a more restricted state-dependent controller might stabilize it. Failure to obtain a positive common margin does not by itself establish instability.

\subsection{What follows for the exact plant}

The exact conditioned cycle map retains a state-dependent timing remainder. Since $\|\dtau^{\mathrm{app}}\|\le\|K\|\|\vect{e}\|$, its normalized local form is
\begin{equation}
    \vect{z}_{k+1}=A(\beta_k)\vect{z}_k+\mathcal O(\|\vect{z}_k\|^2).
\end{equation}
The common-$P$ proof does not bound that remainder over a large-error region.

There is nevertheless a local exact result. Strict nominal dwell margin places a neighborhood of $\vect{e}=\vect{0}$ inside $\mathcal C$. There the conditioner is inactive, and the exact closed-loop map is smooth with Jacobian $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$. The endpoint inequalities imply that this Jacobian is Schur. The discrete-time linearization theorem therefore gives local exponential stability of the cycle anchor, in a sufficiently small invariant neighborhood. Smooth interval flows and switching offsets tending to zero extend convergence to the phase-aligned reference periodic trajectory between cycle starts. This argument does not claim nonlinear invariance of the entire polyhedron $\mathcal C$.

Open loop is a distinct case. With fixed nominal durations, \eqref{eq:augmented-cycle-block-form} gives $\mathcal P(\vect{x},\vect{0})=\Phi\vect{x}+\vect{h}$ exactly. Subtracting its fixed point yields
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=\Phi\vect{e}_k
    \label{eq:exact-open-loop}
\end{equation}
without a linearization remainder. Thus a Schur $\Phi$ proves convergence under the prescribed open-loop schedule throughout this ideal affine model. This fact does not extend to the nonlinear timing-feedback map.

\section{Application to a three-cell converter}
\label{sec:case-study}

The application checks the cycle model, evaluates the common-$P$ certificate, and compares exact controlled and open-loop trajectories. Since the benchmark is already open-loop stable, the practical question is how much feedback accelerates convergence while preserving dwell feasibility.

\subsection{Benchmark and controller parameters}

We use the three-cell multilevel DC--DC converter benchmark of Patino et al. \cite{patino2010}, with $\vect{x}=[v_{C_1},v_{C_2},i_L]^{\mathsf T}$. For binary switch commands $u_1,u_2,u_3\in\{0,1\}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&(u_2-u_1)/C_1\\
0&0&(u_3-u_2)/C_2\\
(u_1-u_2)/L&(u_2-u_3)/L&-R/L
\end{bmatrix},\\
\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\Eu_3/L\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:converter}
\end{equation}
Both matrices depend on the switch state, so the common-state-matrix reduction in \eqref{eq:marcolino-specialization} does not apply. The fixed nine-interval physical mode sequence is $(0,1,3,7,2,0,4,7,4)$, where the physical mode ID is $4u_1+2u_2+u_3$.

The published cycle boundaries are $(0,66,88,110,132,154,220,242,264,286)$~\si{\micro\second}, with anchor $\vect{x}^{\star}=(9.9247,19.2928,0.9823)^{\mathsf T}$. These rounded data do not close exactly. A one-time least-squares dwell reconciliation preserves the reported anchor, \SI{\NominalPeriodMicroseconds}{\micro\second} period, and \SI{\BenchmarkDwellBound}{\micro\second} nominal-design bound. It gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \bar{\vect{t}}={}&(0,63.889,87.557,109.557,132.538,154.538,\\
    &218.210,240.380,263.828,286)~\si{\micro\second}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:benchmark-schedule}
\end{equation}
Computations use the full-precision schedule, with closure error \NominalClosureError{} in the infinity norm and maximum boundary correction \MaximumBoundaryCorrection{}~\si{\micro\second}. This reconciliation prepares the benchmark; it is not a proposed reference-trajectory design method.

\subsection{Model checks and numerical Lyapunov certificate}

Central finite differences of the exact map check both Jacobians. State steps are $10^{-4}$ times the diagonal entries of $S_x$, and the timing step is $10^{-3}t_s=\SI{0.01}{\micro\second}$. The relative errors for $\Phi$ and $\Gamma_\tau$ are \PhiJacobianRelativeError{} and \GammaJacobianRelativeError{}, respectively. For simultaneous normalized state and timing perturbations of amplitude $\epsilon$, we also evaluate
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    r_{\mathrm{lin}}(\epsilon)=\bigl\|S_x^{-1}\bigl[&\mathcal P(\vect{x}^{\star}+\Delta\vect{x},\dtau)-\vect{x}^{\star}\\
    &-\Phi\Delta\vect{x}-\Gamma_\tau\dtau\bigr]\bigr\|_2.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The fitted log--log slope is \LinearizationResidualSlope{}, consistent with the second-order remainder.

For the aggressive gain, the Julia implementation solves \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} using Convex.jl and Clarabel with $\operatorname{tr}(P)=3$. It returns optimal status and $\eta=\num{\LyapunovEta}$. The common matrix, displayed to six decimal places, is
\begin{equation}
    P\approx\LyapunovMatrix.
    \label{eq:numerical-p}
\end{equation}
Full-precision eigenvalue checks give
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{\min}(P)&=\num{\LyapunovMinEigenvalue},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_0)&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointZeroDecrease},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_1)&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointOneDecrease}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:numerical-margins}
\end{equation}
Using the verified $\mu=\num{\LyapunovDecrease}$ in \eqref{eq:uniform-bound} gives $r=\LyapunovRateBound$. This worst-case bound accommodates arbitrarily weak correction and is correspondingly slow. It is not a prediction of the aggressive controller's observed settling time.

The Julia solve uses endpoint matrices from a Python reconstruction of the physical model, rather than independently reconstructing the converter. The article pipeline compares those endpoints against its MATLAB model and aggressive gain, then recomputes the inequalities using that model and the returned $P$. The machine-readable certificate retains full precision; the displayed rounded matrix is checked separately. These are floating-point numerical checks, not interval-arithmetic certification.

\section{Discussion}

Uniform conditioning connects two properties that must be distinguished. The dwell inequalities give a closed-form maximal feasible scale for any finite raw action. The common quadratic Lyapunov inequalities then give a sufficient convergence certificate for the associated linearized matrix segment. Both the SDP and the supporting invariant-region computation are offline. Online execution remains a static gain multiplication followed by adjacent differences and a minimum scan.

The one-cycle sensitivity follows Patino et al. \cite{patino2010}, and the common-state-matrix reduction recovers Marcolino et al.'s fixed-period timing model \cite{marcolino2021}. Direction-preserving scaling is also established in control allocation \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. The contribution here is the closed-form specialization to coupled dwell inequalities and its connection to a common-$P$ certificate for conditioned timing feedback, not a new LQR or a new general Lyapunov theorem.

The full-interval certificate is conservative. It must include the slow open-loop endpoint and use the same quadratic function everywhere. It neither proves that every stabilizing timing gain admits such a certificate nor bounds the nonlinear remainder at arbitrary errors. For the benchmark, open loop already approaches the reference, so the benefit of feedback is faster convergence with feasible schedules. Claims of global nonlinear stabilization by the conditioned controller would go beyond these results.

The assumed \SI{\AppliedScheduleDwellBound}{\micro\second} bound has not been calibrated to hardware. Timer quantization, delays, dead time, implementation margin, state estimation, parameter uncertainty, and experiments remain outside the study. The mode sequence, period, and reference phase are fixed throughout.

\section{Conclusion}

The proposed conditioner applies the largest dwell-feasible scalar multiple of a proportional timing action in $O(N)$ time, preserving its direction and the fixed cycle period. A common quadratic Lyapunov function found through an offline endpoint SDP certifies uniform exponential stability of the conditioned linearized model for every conditioning-factor sequence in $[0,1]$. Strict nominal dwell margin also yields a local exact nonlinear stability result. In the converter application, exact 3D trajectories and error histories show that conditioning makes the aggressive action feasible and accelerates convergence relative to an already stable open-loop schedule. The long-horizon simulation confirms convergence to periodic motion. Extending the feedback guarantee beyond a local nonlinear neighborhood requires an explicit remainder or nonlinear Lyapunov analysis.

\section{Feasible-region expansion under timing-action scaling}
\label{app:invariant-region}

\subsection{Expansion of the dwell-feasible region}

Reducing the fraction of a timing command makes that command feasible for larger cycle-start errors. For a fixed factor $\beta\in(0,1]$, the action $\dtau=-\beta K\vect{e}$ satisfies the dwell bounds on
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal F_\beta&=\{\vect{e}:\beta DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}
    =\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal C,\\
    \mathcal F_1&=\mathcal C.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fixed-beta-feasible}
\end{equation}
Thus every radial extent grows by $1/\beta$ relative to the raw-action admissible region. For $0<\beta_a<\beta_b\le1$, $\mathcal F_{\beta_b}\subseteq\mathcal F_{\beta_a}$. In the three-state benchmark, the volume grows by $\beta^{-3}$. The factors $\LowerComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, $\MiddleComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, and $1$ therefore give volume ratios $\LowerComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, $\MiddleComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, and $1$, respectively. This scaling follows from the dwell inequalities; by itself it is a current-cycle feasibility result, not a stability claim.

\subsection{Feasibility over successive cycles}

Current-cycle feasibility does not alone guarantee that the next error remains in the same feasible region. Holding a comparison factor $\beta$ fixed gives the linearized physical-error dynamics
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=M_\beta\vect{e}_k,\qquad
    M_\beta=\Phi-\beta\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
For each of the three plotted factors, we checked the positive-invariance condition
\begin{equation}
    M_\beta\mathcal F_\beta\subseteq\mathcal F_\beta.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-invariance}
\end{equation}
The offline polyhedral calculation uses MPT3 \cite{herceg2013mpt3} and the preimage-containment approach of \cite{gilbert1991maximal}. All three regions pass for this benchmark. Equivalently, each vertex $\vect{v}$ satisfies $\beta DK M_\beta\vect{v}\le\vect{m}$ within numerical tolerance. Linearity and convexity extend the vertex checks to every point in the polyhedron, so repeated application of the same fixed-factor controller keeps its linearized trajectory dwell-feasible.

Since $S_x^{-1}M_\beta S_x=A(\beta)$, \cref{thm:common-p} also guarantees convergence of each fixed-factor linearized model. Thus every initial error in a plotted region remains feasible and converges under its corresponding fixed factor. The case $\beta=1$ is simply the raw controller on $\mathcal F_1=\mathcal C$; the smaller factors admit larger regions of feasible linearized operation.

These region checks concern fixed factors, whereas the simulated controller selects $\beta_k$ from the current error. They do not establish nonlinear invariance of the plotted polyhedra or an enlarged region of attraction for the exact plant. Exact nonlinear stability remains local, and the displayed large-error trajectory is simulation evidence. The common-$P$ theorem covers the conditioned linearized family independently of these polyhedral checks.

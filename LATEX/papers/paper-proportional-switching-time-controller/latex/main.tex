\title[Dwell-conditioned switching-time feedback]{Dwell-Conditioned Cycle-to-Cycle Switching-Time Feedback for Mode-Dependent Switched-Affine Systems}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Daniel} \sur{Vieira}}\email{danielvie@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Roberto Kawakami Harrop} \sur{Galv\~ao}}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Systems and Control}, \orgname{Instituto Tecnol\'ogico de Aeron\'autica}, \orgaddress{\city{S\~ao Jos\'e dos Campos}, \state{S\~ao Paulo}, \country{Brazil}}}

\abstract{
This paper adresses the problem of controlling a switching-time affine system under dwell-time constraints. The goal is to steer the system to a target periodic trajectory rather than a constant average operating point. We propose a proportional controller and a signal conditioner to comply with the minimum dwell-time constraints. The method combines a solver-free enforcement of the minimum dwell-time constraints with a common quadratic Lyapunov function approach. The controller acts by generating the delta time to be applyed on the switching instants, then conditioning the signal by reducing the requested adjustiments when the schedule is not feasible. The example used here is a three-cell DC-DC converter, we verify the stability of the linearized model using this approach. The simulations show faster convergence of this method compared to a conservative feedback and open-loop opearation while respecting the dwell-time contraints. The analysis guarantees exponential stability of the lineared model even when the conditioning factor changes from cyclo to other.
}

\keywords{switched-affine systems, periodic trajectories, switching-time control, dwell-time conditioning, common quadratic Lyapunov function, semidefinite programming}

\section{Introduction}

Power converters control voltage and current by moving through a sequence of switch configurations. In practice, the switching pattern must respect electrical limits, switching frequency, a minimum dwell duration for each configuration, and a short computation time \cite{karamanakos2020mpcPowerElectronics}. Keeping the individual configurations explicit gives a hybrid-affine or switched-affine model \cite{albea2021hybridAffinePwm}. Repeating the pattern produces a periodic trajectory rather than a constant state, and controlling such trajectories is an established problem for switched-affine systems \cite{egidio2020,xu2024fcsLimitCycle}.

Predictive control tackles the problem by solving a constrained optimization problem online. Recent work reduces the online burden through Laguerre parameterization, dedicated fixed-frequency solvers, and candidate pruning \cite{saeed2022laguerre,yang2022fixed,augustine2023mpc}. Controllers also differ in what they may change. State-dependent switching laws choose the active subsystem \cite{egidio2022rank}, where switching-time control keeps an ordered sequence and moves its transition instants. We take this same approach, changing the transition instants and keepping the rest of the parameters the same. At each cycle start, the error is computed and the offsets applied during that cycle, with no online solver.

Patino et al. studied mode-dependent cyclic systems and how each switching instant affects the trajectory. They applied an online predictive controller to adjust the switching instants and track an optimal periodic trajectory \cite{patino2010}. Marcolino et al. considered a fixed-period cycle controlled by changing its interior switching instants to regulate a target point on the periodic trajectory, called the cycle anchor in this paper. They used a discrete LQR and dwell-constrained predictive control to apply corrections based on the one-cycle model \cite{marcolino2021}. Their work considers a linear model that has a common state matrix with switched actuator levels. Each interior switching instant separates two dwell durations, corresponding to the modes active before and after it. Delaying the instant lengthens the preceding dwell and shortens the following one, so the dwell limits are coupled. The predictive controller enforces these limits through online optimization, and its feasible set is characterized through polyhedral projection \cite{marcolino2021}. The standalone LQR comparison instead clips the offsets one at a time, which changes the requested direction and may shift the final scheduled instant \cite{marcolino2021}. Projecting a raw action directly onto the feasible set would require a quadratic program and would generally change its direction. Direction-preserving control allocation scales the complete command, but it also uses online optimization and does not address fixed-period dwell constraints \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. The remaining problem is to find the largest feasible scale directly, preserving the timing-action direction, mode order, and cycle period without an online solver.

We adapt direction-preserving scaling to fixed-period switching schedules. Each dwell constraint gives an upper bound on the scale, and the smallest bound is the largest feasible factor in $[0,1]$. Checking all $N$ bounds takes $O(N)$ time and requires no online solver. Scaling the complete raw offset vector preserves its direction, the mode order, and the cycle period. For the linearized raw closed loop, an offline polyhedral iteration finds the maximal invariant subset of the Raw-Action Admissible Region. Outside this set, the conditioner guarantees current-cycle dwell feasibility but not convergence; the nonlinear guarantee remains local. \Cref{sec:model} derives the cycle model, \cref{sec:conditioning} develops the conditioner and invariant region, and \cref{sec:case-study} presents the converter study.

\section{Problem formulation and one-cycle model}
\label{sec:model}

The control objective is to approach a prescribed periodic trajectory without changing its mode sequence or cycle period. We first express this objective at cycle starts, then derive how shifts of the interior switching instants change the next cycle-start state.

\subsection{Plant, reference trajectory, and timing coordinates}

Consider the switched-affine plant
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vect{x}}(t)=A_{\sigma(t)}\vect{x}(t)+\vect{b}_{\sigma(t)},
    \qquad \vect{x}(t)\in\R^n.
    \label{eq:plant}
\end{equation}
Every cycle visits the fixed sequence $(q_1,\ldots,q_N)$. Its nominal cycle-relative boundaries and dwell durations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    0&=\bar t_0<\bar t_1<\cdots<\bar t_N=T,\\
    \bar d_i&=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Repeating this schedule generates the reference periodic trajectory $\bar{\vect{x}}(t)$. Its cycle anchor is $\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=\bar{\vect{x}}(T)$. With $\vect{x}_k=\vect{x}(kT)$, the cycle-start error is $\vect{e}_k=\vect{x}_k-\bar{\vect{x}}(0)$. The reference schedule and trajectory are given design data. The target is this phase-aligned trajectory, not a constant average operating point.

The controller moves only the $N-1$ interior instants:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    t_{k,i}&=\bar t_i+\delta\tau_{k,i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\\
    \delta\tau_{k,0}&=\delta\tau_{k,N}=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
During interval $i$ of cycle $k$, the plant uses mode $q_i$ between the absolute times $kT+t_{k,i-1}$ and $kT+t_{k,i}$. The applied dwell vector satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{d}_k&=\bar{\vect{d}}+\ddwell_k,\qquad \ddwell_k=D\dtau_k,\\
    D_{ij}&=\begin{cases}
        1,&i=j,\\
        -1,&i=j+1,\\
        0,&\text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:incidence-map}
\end{equation}
where $D\in\R^{N\times(N-1)}$. Thus delaying one instant lengthens the preceding interval and shortens the following one by the same amount. Since $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$, every offset vector preserves $T$. The remaining requirement is $\vect{d}_k\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}$ for a specified $d_{\min}>0$.

\Cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(a)--(b) illustrates the timing coordinates using the converter schedule introduced in \cref{sec:case-study}. In panel (b), delaying instant 8 by \SI{12}{\micro\second} lengthens dwell 8 and shortens dwell 9 by the same amount, with all other offsets zero. The shortened dwell remains above the assumed \AppliedScheduleDwellBound{}~\si{\micro\second} bound. This isolated offset illustrates the dwell coupling; panel (c) instead shows the aggressive controller's first-cycle raw request.

\subsection{Exact augmented propagation and terminal error}

Introduce the augmented state and matrix
\begin{equation}
    \X(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}(t)\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \widetilde A_q=
    \begin{bmatrix}A_q&\vect{b}_q\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&0\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:augmented-generator}
\end{equation}
The affine system becomes the homogeneous system
\begin{equation}
    \dot\X(t)=\widetilde A_{\sigma(t)}\X(t).
\end{equation}

For this derivation, consider one cycle in cycle-relative time. Suppress the cycle index by writing $t_i=t_{k,i}$ and $\vect{x}(t)$ for $\vect{x}(kT+t)$. The endpoints remain fixed: $t_0=\bar t_0=0$ and $t_N=\bar t_N=T$. The mode on interval $[t_{i-1},t_i)$ is $q_i$.

For each interval, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Delta t_i&=t_i-t_{i-1},\\
    \Delta\bar t_i&=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1},\\
    \Delta t_i&=\Delta\bar t_i+\delta t_i,\qquad F_i=\widetilde A_{q_i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:duration-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta t_i=d_{k,i}$, $\Delta\bar t_i=\bar d_i$, and $\delta t_i=\delta d_{k,i}$. Thus $\delta t_i$ is a duration change, not a switching-instant offset.
The state transition on interval $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \X(t_i)=e^{F_i\Delta t_i}\X(t_{i-1}).
\end{equation}
Therefore, the exact cycle propagation is
\begin{equation}
    \X(t_N)=e^{F_N\Delta t_N}e^{F_{N-1}\Delta t_{N-1}}
    \cdots e^{F_1\Delta t_1}\X(t_0).
    \label{eq:exact-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
The first interval is the rightmost factor. Matrices belonging to different modes cannot generally be interchanged.

Define the nominal transition matrix for interval $i$ by
\begin{equation}
    \phi_i=e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}.
    \label{eq:nominal-interval-transition}
\end{equation}
The nominal augmented trajectory is $\bar\X(t)=[\bar{\vect{x}}(t)^{\mathsf T}\ 1]^{\mathsf T}$, so its terminal state satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \bar\X(t_N)=\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
    \label{eq:nominal-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
Both terminal states are evaluated at the same fixed time $T$.

Let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t)&=\X(t)-\bar\X(t),\\
    \X(t_0)&=\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\vect{e}(t)\in\R^{n+1}$ is the augmented error, whose last component is zero. The physical cycle-start error $\vect{e}_k\in\R^n$ contains only the first $n$ components at the cycle start. Subtracting \eqref{eq:nominal-augmented-cycle} from \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-cycle} gives the exact terminal error
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)={}&
    e^{F_N(\Delta\bar t_N+\delta t_N)}
    e^{F_{N-1}(\Delta\bar t_{N-1}+\delta t_{N-1})}\cdots\\
    &\quad e^{F_1(\Delta\bar t_1+\delta t_1)}
    \bigl(\bar\X(t_0)+\vect{e}(t_0)\bigr)\\
    &-\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:exact-augmented-error}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Linearization in the interval durations}

For a constant matrix $F_i$, expand $e^{F_i s}$ around $s=\Delta\bar t_i$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}e^{F_i s}&=F_i e^{F_i s},\\
    e^{F_i(\Delta\bar t_i+\delta t_i)}
    &=e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}
      +F_i e^{F_i\Delta\bar t_i}\,\delta t_i
      +\mathcal O(\delta t_i^2)\\
    &=\phi_i+F_i\phi_i\,\delta t_i+\mathcal O(\delta t_i^2)\\
    &\doteq\phi_i+F_i\phi_i\,\delta t_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:interval-first-order}
\end{equation}
Here $\doteq$ denotes equality up to first order around zero initial error and zero duration changes. Terms containing two duration changes or a duration change multiplied by the initial error are second order.

Substituting the first-order approximation into every transition gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    &e^{F_N(\Delta\bar t_N+\delta t_N)}
    e^{F_{N-1}(\Delta\bar t_{N-1}+\delta t_{N-1})}\cdots\\
    &\qquad e^{F_1(\Delta\bar t_1+\delta t_1)}\\
    &\doteq
    (\phi_N+F_N\phi_N\,\delta t_N)
    (\phi_{N-1}+F_{N-1}\phi_{N-1}\,\delta t_{N-1})\\
    &\qquad\quad\cdots(\phi_1+F_1\phi_1\,\delta t_1).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:product-before-expansion}
\end{equation}
Multiplying these factors while keeping at most one duration change per term yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    &(\phi_N+F_N\phi_N\,\delta t_N)\cdots
    (\phi_1+F_1\phi_1\,\delta t_1)\\
    &\quad\doteq\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\\
    &\qquad{}+F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\,\delta t_N\\
    &\qquad{}+\phi_NF_{N-1}\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\,\delta t_{N-1}\\
    &\qquad{}+\cdots\\
    &\qquad{}+\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\,\delta t_1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ordered-product-first-order}
\end{equation}
Each inserted $F_i$ stays next to its own nominal transition $\phi_i$, with the other interval transitions in their original order.

Substitute \eqref{eq:ordered-product-first-order} into \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-error}. The nominal term $\phi_N\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)$ cancels. Discarding the products $\delta t_i\vect{e}(t_0)$ then gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq{}&
    \phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\vect{e}(t_0)\\
    &+\phi_N\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_1\\
    &+\phi_N\cdots\phi_3F_2\phi_2\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_2\\
    &+\cdots\\
    &+F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\delta t_N.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:expanded-terminal-error}
\end{equation}

Collect the duration changes and their sensitivity columns:
\begin{equation}
    \delta\vect{t}=
    \begin{bmatrix}\delta t_1&\delta t_2&\cdots&\delta t_N\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Gamma=\Bigl[\,&
    \phi_N\cdots\phi_2F_1\phi_1\bar\X(t_0),\\
    &\phi_N\cdots\phi_3F_2\phi_2\phi_1\bar\X(t_0),\ \ldots,\\
    &F_N\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1\bar\X(t_0)\,\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:augmented-dwell-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
The columns are concatenated horizontally in the same order as the entries of $\delta\vect{t}$, giving $\Gamma\in\R^{(n+1)\times N}$. For the final compact form, define the complete nominal transition $\Phi\in\R^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}$ by
\begin{equation}
    \Phi=\phi_N\phi_{N-1}\cdots\phi_1.
    \label{eq:augmented-nominal-matrix}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:expanded-terminal-error} is thus
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}(t_N)\doteq\Phi\vect{e}(t_0)+\Gamma\delta\vect{t}.
    \label{eq:augmented-error-linearization}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Physical model and independent switching instants}

We now extract the physical error and return to cycle-indexed notation. Subtracting two augmented states gives an error with last coordinate zero. Define
\begin{equation}
    \Pi=\begin{bmatrix}I_n&\vect{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \iota=\begin{bmatrix}I_n\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Periodicity gives $\bar{\vect{x}}(t_N)=\bar{\vect{x}}(t_0)=\bar{\vect{x}}(0)$, so $\vect{e}(t_0)=\iota\vect{e}_k$ and $\Pi\vect{e}(t_N)=\vect{e}_{k+1}$. With $\delta\vect{t}=\ddwell_k$, projection of \eqref{eq:augmented-error-linearization} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}&\doteq\Phi_x\vect{e}_k+G_d\ddwell_k,\\
    \Phi_x&=\Pi\Phi\iota
    =e^{A_{q_N}\bar d_N}\cdots e^{A_{q_1}\bar d_1},\\
    G_d&=\Pi\Gamma.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:physical-dwell-model}
\end{equation}
The physical nominal cycle matrix is $\Phi_x\in\R^{n\times n}$, whereas $\Phi$ remains the augmented transition. The physical dwell-sensitivity matrix is $G_d\in\R^{n\times N}$. From here onward, $\vect{e}_k$, or $\vect{e}$ when the cycle index is omitted, denotes the physical error in $\R^n$.

The $N$ duration changes are not independent under the fixed-period constraint. They sum to zero and satisfy $\ddwell_k=D\dtau_k$, as defined in \eqref{eq:incidence-map}. Substitution gives the model in the controller's $N-1$ independent switching-instant offsets, with its remainder written explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}={}&\Phi_x\vect{e}_k+\Gamma_\tau\dtau_k\\
    &+\mathcal O\bigl((\|\vect{e}_k\|+\|\dtau_k\|)^2\bigr),\\
    \Gamma_\tau={}&G_dD.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-model}
\end{equation}
Here $\Gamma_\tau\in\R^{n\times(N-1)}$ maps the independent timing offsets to the physical error. The remainder is understood in fixed state and timing units. The exact physical one-cycle map underlying this approximation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal P(\vect{x},\dtau)={}&\Pi e^{F_N[\bar d_N+(D\dtau)_N]}\cdots\\
    &\quad e^{F_1[\bar d_1+(D\dtau)_1]}
    \begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:exact-cycle-map}
\end{equation}
The nominal schedule satisfies $\mathcal P(\bar{\vect{x}}(0),\vect{0})=\bar{\vect{x}}(0)$. The augmented nominal product has the block form
\begin{equation}
    \Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_x&\vect{h}\\\vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&1\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:augmented-cycle-block-form}
\end{equation}
Its unit eigenvalue comes from the auxiliary constant coordinate. Stability is tested on the physical error dynamics, not on the full augmented matrix.

Each column of $D$ subtracts two adjacent dwell-sensitivity columns. Factoring this difference gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}={}&e^{A_{q_N}\bar d_N}\cdots e^{A_{q_{i+1}}\bar d_{i+1}}\\
    &\times\bigl[(A_{q_i}-A_{q_{i+1}})\bar{\vect{x}}(\bar t_i)
    +\vect{b}_{q_i}-\vect{b}_{q_{i+1}}\bigr].
\end{split}
\label{eq:timing-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The bracket is the change in the affine vector field caused by trading time between adjacent modes. The remaining transitions carry this effect to the cycle end. This recovers the switching-sensitivity structure in \cite{patino2010}; switching-time sensitivities also appear in the hybrid limit-cycle analysis of \cite{flieller2006limitCycles}.

For the special case $A_{q_i}=A_c$ and $\vect{b}_{q_i}=B_c\vect{u}_{i-1}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_x=e^{A_cT},\quad
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}=e^{A_c(T-\bar t_i)}B_c(\vect{u}_{i-1}-\vect{u}_i),
    \label{eq:marcolino-specialization}
\end{equation}
which is the fixed-period switched-actuator model of \cite{marcolino2021}, with the corresponding input-jump sign convention. Mode-dependent state matrices require the full expression in \eqref{eq:timing-jacobian}.

\subsection{Raw proportional timing feedback}

A fixed gain $K\in\R^{(N-1)\times n}$ requests
\begin{equation}
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k,\qquad
    A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\Phi_x-\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:raw-law}
\end{equation}
The gain can be designed from \eqref{eq:cycle-model}, for example by discrete LQR. Here the gain is fixed before conditioning and certification. Although a Schur $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ gives a locally stabilizing raw law, it does not make every requested schedule admissible. In \cref{fig:timing-mechanism}(c), the raw request moves the last interior instant beyond $T$, making $d_N=T-t_{k,N-1}<0$. A minimum dwell constraint is stricter still: it requires $t_{k,N-1}\le T-d_{\min}$ even before the boundaries cross. The next section enforces all such adjacent-dwell constraints; \cref{sec:stability} examines the resulting conditioned dynamics.

\section{Solver-free dwell-time conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

The conditioner determines how much of the raw timing action can be applied without making any dwell duration shorter than $d_{\min}$. It uses one factor $\beta_k\in[0,1]$ for all switching-instant offsets. A factor of one keeps the complete request; a smaller factor moves each requested instant back toward its nominal position. Because moving one instant changes two adjacent dwell durations, the factor must respect all intervals.

\subsection{Computing the conditioning factor}

First, calculate how much each nominal interval can be shortened. This available margin is
\begin{equation}
    \vect{m}=\bar{\vect{d}}-d_{\min}\vect{1}>\vect{0}.
    \label{eq:strict-margin}
\end{equation}
We assume strictly positive margins, so the nominal schedule satisfies every dwell constraint before control is applied.

Next, take adjacent differences of the raw switching-instant offsets to obtain the requested dwell changes,
\[
    \vect{q}_k=D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}.
\]
If $q_{k,i}\ge0$, interval $i$ stays unchanged or becomes longer and imposes no restriction. If $q_{k,i}<0$, the request shortens that interval by $-q_{k,i}$. After scaling, the shortening is $\beta(-q_{k,i})$ and must not exceed $m_i$. Thus this interval allows at most the fraction $m_i/(-q_{k,i})$ of the raw action. For example, a requested shortening of twice the available margin limits the factor to one half.

The largest factor allowed by all intervals is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \beta_k&=\min\left\{1,\ \min_{i:q_{k,i}<0}\frac{m_i}{-q_{k,i}}\right\},\\
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}&=\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioner}
\end{equation}
The following scan evaluates this factor without trial schedules or an optimization solver:
\begin{quote}
\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{1em}\=\hspace{1em}\=\kill
$\beta_k\gets1$ \\
\textbf{for} $i=1,\ldots,N$ \textbf{do} \\
\>\textbf{if} $q_{k,i}<0$ \textbf{then} \\
\>\>$\mathit{limit}\gets m_i/(-q_{k,i})$ \\
\>\>$\beta_k\gets\min\{\beta_k,\mathit{limit}\}$ \\
\>\textbf{end if} \\
\textbf{end for} \\
$\dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}\gets\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}$
\end{tabbing}
\end{quote}
A feasible raw request leaves $\beta_k=1$. Otherwise, the smallest ratio reduces the complete action until at least one dwell reaches $d_{\min}$. The empty minimum in \eqref{eq:conditioner} is taken as $+\infty$, consistent with leaving the factor at one when no interval is shortened.

\subsection{Feasibility and maximality}

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility and maximality]
\label{prop:conditioner}
Under \eqref{eq:strict-margin}, the factor in \eqref{eq:conditioner} satisfies
\[
    \bar{\vect{d}}+\beta_k D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}.
\]
It is the largest factor in $[0,1]$ that satisfies all dwell constraints. For a finite raw action, $\beta_k>0$. The conditioner preserves the direction of a nonzero raw action, the mode order, and the cycle period. The factor can be computed in $O(N)$ time without an online solver.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each shortened interval imposes the upper bound derived above. Their minimum, capped at one, satisfies every bound. Any larger factor in $[0,1]$ would violate at least one of them. This proves feasibility and maximality.

Every ratio $m_i/(-q_{k,i})$ used in \eqref{eq:conditioner} is positive for a finite raw action. Hence $\beta_k>0$, and multiplying the complete vector by this factor preserves its direction. The identity $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$ ensures that the dwell changes sum to zero, so the period remains $T$. Every applied dwell is at least $d_{\min}>0$, so the cycle boundaries remain ordered.

Finally, $\vect{q}_k$ is computed by adjacent differences of the raw offsets. A single scan through the $N$ dwell changes finds the smallest bound. Both operations take $O(N)$ time.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Online execution and admissible region}

In the cycle model used here, the controller reads the cycle-start state, computes the error and raw offsets, then runs the scan above. It calculates one $\beta_k$ for the complete schedule, not a new factor at each switching instant. Only the conditioned schedule is applied to the plant. The model assigns no computation delay to these steps.

The errors for which no reduction is needed form the Raw-Action Admissible Region. Substituting $\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k$ into the dwell constraints gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C=\{\vect{e}:DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}.
    \label{eq:admissible-region}
\end{equation}
Inside $\mathcal C$, the raw action is feasible and $\beta_k=1$. Outside it, at least one requested dwell is too short and $\beta_k<1$. The scan already handles both cases, so no separate region-membership test is needed. \Cref{app:invariant-region} compares the feasible regions for smaller fixed factors and checks their invariance under the corresponding linearized dynamics.

\section{Lyapunov stability and convex optimization}
\label{sec:stability}

The conditioner keeps the dwell durations feasible, but feasibility alone does not imply convergence. We now examine the linearized dynamics as the conditioning factor changes. The argument first identifies the two endpoint matrices, then uses one Lyapunov function to cover every factor between them. We then formulate an offline certificate computation for a fixed gain.

\subsection{Conditioned matrix family}

We work directly with the physical cycle-start error $\vect{e}_k$. The two endpoint matrices are the nominal cycle matrix and the raw closed-loop matrix:
\begin{equation}
    A_0=\Phi_x,\qquad A_1=A_{\mathrm{cl}}.
    \label{eq:endpoints}
\end{equation}
The conditioner applies a fraction $\beta_k$ of the raw action. Substituting this action into the first-order model gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}&=A(\beta_k)\vect{e}_k,\\
    A(\beta)&=(1-\beta)A_0+\beta A_1
    =\Phi_x-\beta\Gamma_\tau K.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioned-family}
\end{equation}
At $\beta=0$, the matrix is $\Phi_x$, corresponding to no timing feedback. At $\beta=1$, it is $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$, corresponding to the complete raw action. Intermediate factors give matrices on the segment joining these endpoints.

Equation~\eqref{eq:conditioned-family} is a linearized model. It omits the remainder in \eqref{eq:cycle-model}. We certify it on the closed interval $[0,1]$. The implemented conditioner returns a positive factor for every finite error, but including zero also covers the limit of arbitrarily weak feedback.

Even if both endpoints are Schur, the matrices between them need not all be stable. Stability of each matrix would also not, by itself, guarantee stability when the factor changes from cycle to cycle. We therefore seek one quadratic Lyapunov function that decreases for both endpoints and remains valid as $\beta_k$ changes.

\subsection{Common quadratic Lyapunov certificate}

Using one quadratic function for a family of discrete-time systems is the common quadratic Lyapunov function approach \cite{mason2004commonQuadratic}.

Consider $V(\vect{e})=\vect{e}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{e}$ with $P\succ0$. The endpoint inequalities below require this function to decrease by at least $\eta\|\vect{e}\|_2^2$ in one cycle. The same $P$ and the same positive margin $\eta$ must work at both endpoints.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.96\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/common_p_geometry.tex}}
    \caption{Common-$P$ schematic in error coordinates. Both endpoint images lie inside the unit level set of $V$. Every intermediate image $A(\beta)\vect{e}$ lies on the segment joining them.}
    \label{fig:common-p}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform stability under conditioning]
\label{thm:common-p}
Suppose there exist $P=P^{\mathsf T}\succ0$ and $\eta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}
    P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i\succeq\eta I_n,\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:endpoint-lmis}
\end{equation}
Then $V(\vect{e})=\vect{e}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{e}$ decreases for every nonzero state of \eqref{eq:conditioned-family} and every $\beta\in[0,1]$. The conditioned linearized model is uniformly exponentially stable for every sequence $\{\beta_k\}\subset[0,1]$, including the state-dependent factors in \eqref{eq:conditioner}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First, express the desired decrease $A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)-P\prec0$ as a block-matrix inequality. For the same $P\succ0$ at both endpoints, define
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal S(\beta)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}\\
        A(\beta)&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:schur-block}
\end{equation}
The lower-right block is negative definite. Its Schur complement is $-P+A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)$, so
\[
    \mathcal S(\beta)\prec0
    \Longleftrightarrow
    A(\beta)^{\mathsf T}PA(\beta)-P\prec0.
\]
The endpoint assumptions therefore make both $\mathcal S(0)$ and $\mathcal S(1)$ negative definite.

Next, use $A(\beta)=(1-\beta)A_0+\beta A_1$. Since the same $P$ is used throughout, the diagonal blocks do not change with $\beta$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal S(\beta)
    &=(1-\beta)\begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A_0^{\mathsf T}\\
        A_0&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}\\
    &\quad+\beta\begin{bmatrix}
        -P&A_1^{\mathsf T}\\
        A_1&-P^{-1}
    \end{bmatrix}\prec0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:schur-convex-combination}
\end{equation}
Both endpoint blocks are negative definite, and their weights are nonnegative and sum to one. Their convex combination is therefore negative definite for every $\beta\in[0,1]$.

Taking the Schur complement shows that $V(\vect{e})=\vect{e}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{e}$ decreases strictly for every nonzero error and every $\beta\in[0,1]$. The same $P$ applies throughout the interval. Since the matrix family depends continuously on $\beta$ over this compact interval, the decrease has a uniform positive margin. The quadratic Lyapunov criterion therefore establishes uniform exponential stability for any conditioning-factor sequence.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:common-p} gives a geometric interpretation. Start with an error on the level set $V(\vect{e})=1$. Both endpoint images lie strictly inside the same ellipsoid. The intermediate image $A(\beta)\vect{e}$ lies on the line segment joining them, which also stays inside the ellipsoid. Using the same $V$ at every cycle allows this decrease to be carried forward even when $\beta_k$ changes. The drawing is a two-dimensional schematic, not a converter trajectory or a nonlinear region of attraction.

The proof covers the entire interval, not just sampled factors. It applies to all initial errors of the linearized model, including errors outside $\mathcal C$. This does not make the linear approximation valid for arbitrarily large physical errors.

\subsection{Offline semidefinite program}

Related common-matrix vertex-LMI formulations appear in discrete-time robust stability \cite{deOliveira1999lmiDiscrete} and in robust polytopic PWM converter control based on continuous-time averaged models \cite{olalla2009robustLqrPwm}.

The Lyapunov matrix can be computed offline, after the feedback gain $K$ has been chosen. With $A_0=\Phi_x$ and $A_1=A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ fixed, an endpoint SDP is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \underset{P=P^{\mathsf T},\,\eta}{\operatorname{maximize}}\quad&\eta\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
    &\operatorname{tr}(P)=n,\\
    &P\succeq\varepsilon_P I_n,\\
    &P-A_0^{\mathsf T}PA_0\succeq\eta I_n,\\
    &P-A_1^{\mathsf T}PA_1\succeq\eta I_n,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:common-p-sdp}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_P=10^{-7}$. The constraint $P\succeq\varepsilon_P I_n$ imposes a positive-definiteness floor. The trace constraint fixes the otherwise arbitrary positive scale of $P$.

Because the endpoint matrices are fixed, the constraints in \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} are affine in the unknowns $P$ and $\eta$. The block form in \eqref{eq:schur-block} is affine in $\beta$ for a fixed $P$, which is what the proof requires. It is not affine in $P$ because it contains $P^{-1}$. The numerical application uses the inverse-free inequalities in \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp}, expressed in scaled coordinates as described in \cref{sec:case-study}. This optimization finds a certificate for the chosen gain; it does not redesign $K$.

Successful solver termination is not enough to establish a certificate. We first symmetrize the returned $P$, then recompute the endpoint decrease matrices and their smallest eigenvalues:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q_i&=P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i,\qquad i\in\{0,1\},\\
    \mu&=\min\{\lambda_{\min}(Q_0),\lambda_{\min}(Q_1)\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:verified-margin}
\end{equation}
We check that $P$ is positive definite, that its trace has the required value, and that $\mu>0$. A positive verified margin supplies the common decrease required by \cref{thm:common-p}.

This test is sufficient, not necessary. Including $\beta=0$ requires the open-loop endpoint $A_0$ to be Schur. If that endpoint is unstable, the full-interval certificate cannot hold, even if restricting the applied factors might allow stabilization. More generally, failure to find a positive common margin does not establish instability.

The stability theorem applies to the conditioned linearized model. The large-error response of the exact nonlinear system, including the initially conditioned cycles, is evaluated by simulation.

\section{Application to a three-cell converter}
\label{sec:case-study}

We now apply the controller to a three-cell converter. After defining the benchmark and gains, we check the cycle model and the numerical Lyapunov certificate. We then compare exact simulations with and without feedback. The benchmark is already open-loop stable, so the comparison asks how much feedback accelerates convergence while keeping every dwell duration feasible.

\subsection{Benchmark and controller parameters}

We use the three-cell multilevel DC--DC converter benchmark of Patino et al. \cite{patino2010}. The state $\vect{x}=[v_{C_1},v_{C_2},i_L]^{\mathsf T}$ contains the two capacitor voltages and the inductor current. For binary switch commands $u_1,u_2,u_3\in\{0,1\}$, the plant matrices are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&(u_2-u_1)/C_1\\
0&0&(u_3-u_2)/C_2\\
(u_1-u_2)/L&(u_2-u_3)/L&-R/L
\end{bmatrix},\\
\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\Eu_3/L\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:converter}
\end{equation}
Both $A_{\vect{u}}$ and $\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}$ depend on the switch state. The common-state-matrix reduction in \eqref{eq:marcolino-specialization} therefore does not apply. We retain the fixed nine-interval sequence $(0,1,3,7,2,0,4,7,4)$. Each physical mode ID is encoded as $4u_1+2u_2+u_3$.

The published cycle boundaries are $(0,66,88,110,132,154,220,242,264,286)$~\si{\micro\second}, and the reported anchor is $\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=(9.9247,19.2928,0.9823)^{\mathsf T}$. Propagation with these rounded data does not return exactly to the anchor after one cycle.

\begin{equation}

    &218.210,240.380,263.828,286)~\si{\micro\second}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:benchmark-schedule}
\end{equation}
Computations use the full-precision schedule rather than the rounded values displayed here. Its closure error is \NominalClosureError{} in the infinity norm, and the largest boundary correction is \MaximumBoundaryCorrection{}~\si{\micro\second}. This adjustment only prepares the published benchmark for the comparisons. It is not a proposed method for designing reference trajectories.

\subsection{Numerical scaling and feedback gains}

For numerical design and reporting, we express the voltage and current errors relative to chosen scales. Let $S_x$ be the positive diagonal state-scale matrix listed in \cref{tab:parameters}. Define the normalized error and endpoint matrices by
\begin{equation}
    \vect{z}=S_x^{-1}\vect{e},\qquad
    \widehat A_i=S_x^{-1}A_iS_x,\quad i\in\{0,1\}.
    \label{eq:normalized-endpoints}
\end{equation}
This change of coordinates leaves the endpoint eigenvalues unchanged. It is a numerical choice, not a requirement of the stability proof.

Setting $\vect{v}=\dtau/t_s$ gives the normalized timing-input matrix $B_n=S_x^{-1}\Gamma_\tau t_s$. Discrete LQR on $(\widehat A_0,B_n)$ then gives $\vect{v}^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K_n\vect{z}$. The corresponding physical-coordinate gain is $K=t_sK_nS_x^{-1}$.

\Cref{tab:parameters} lists the weights for two tunings. The aggressive law uses conditioning. The conservative law is applied without conditioning and remains dwell-feasible in this experiment. The table also distinguishes the nominal-design dwell bound from the smaller applied bound $d_{\min}$. The latter is a simulation assumption, not a hardware-calibrated limit.

\subsection{Checking the one-cycle model}

We first compare the analytical Jacobians with central finite differences of the exact map. Each state step is $10^{-4}$ times the corresponding diagonal entry of $S_x$. The timing step is $10^{-3}t_s=\SI{0.01}{\micro\second}$. The relative errors are \PhiJacobianRelativeError{} for $\Phi_x$ and \GammaJacobianRelativeError{} for $\Gamma_\tau$.

Next, we check how the linearization residual changes with perturbation size. We apply simultaneous normalized state and timing perturbations of amplitude $\epsilon$ and evaluate
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    r_{\mathrm{lin}}(\epsilon)=\bigl\|S_x^{-1}\bigl[&\mathcal P(\bar{\vect{x}}(0)+\Delta\vect{x},\dtau)-\bar{\vect{x}}(0)\\
    &-\Phi_x\Delta\vect{x}-\Gamma_\tau\dtau\bigr]\bigr\|_2.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
A second-order remainder should give a slope of two when this residual is plotted against $\epsilon$ on logarithmic axes. The fitted slope is \LinearizationResidualSlope{}, consistent with that expectation.

\subsection{Numerical Lyapunov certificate}

For the aggressive gain, we apply \eqref{eq:common-p-sdp} to the normalized endpoints $\widehat A_0$ and $\widehat A_1$. Denote the resulting Lyapunov matrix by $P_n$ and the solver margin by $\eta_n$. Julia solves this problem using Convex.jl and Clarabel, with $\operatorname{tr}(P_n)=3$. The solver returns optimal status and $\eta_n=\num{\LyapunovEta}$. The normalized-coordinate matrix, shown to six decimal places, is
\begin{equation}
    P_n\approx\LyapunovMatrix.
    \label{eq:numerical-p}
\end{equation}
We then check the returned matrix rather than relying on solver status alone. Define the normalized endpoint decrease matrices
\[
    Q_{i,n}=P_n-\widehat A_i^{\mathsf T}P_n\widehat A_i,\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Using full precision, their smallest eigenvalues and that of $P_n$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{\min}(P_n)&=\num{\LyapunovMinEigenvalue},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_{0,n})&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointZeroDecrease},\\
    \lambda_{\min}(Q_{1,n})&=\num{\LyapunovEndpointOneDecrease}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:numerical-margins}
\end{equation}
Both endpoint decrease margins are positive. Their minimum is $\mu_n=\num{\LyapunovDecrease}$. The reported trace and margins refer to normalized coordinates.

To connect these checks to the physical-coordinate theorem, use
\[
\begin{aligned}
    P&=S_x^{-\mathsf T}P_nS_x^{-1},\\
    P-A_i^{\mathsf T}PA_i&=S_x^{-\mathsf T}Q_{i,n}S_x^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
These transformations preserve positive definiteness. Thus the numerical checks provide the physical-coordinate certificate required by \cref{thm:common-p}, without changing the feedback gain or the plant trajectories.

The model checks follow a separate sequence. Python reconstructs the physical model and supplies the normalized endpoint matrices to Julia; Julia does not independently reconstruct the converter. The article pipeline compares these endpoints with the normalized matrices from its MATLAB model and aggressive gain. It then recomputes the Lyapunov inequalities using those matrices and the returned $P_n$.

The machine-readable certificate retains full precision. We also check the rounded matrix displayed in \eqref{eq:numerical-p} separately. All of these checks use floating-point arithmetic, not interval-arithmetic certification.

\subsection{Reading the cycle-start error}

To compare convergence without mixing it with switching ripple, we sample the state at the start of each cycle. We report the dimensionless error
\begin{equation}
    E_k=\bigl\|S_x^{-1}\bigl(\vect{x}(kT)-\bar{\vect{x}}(0)\bigr)\bigr\|_2,

\end{equation}
where $\bar{\vect{x}}(0)$ is the cycle anchor. The matrix $S_x=\operatorname{diag}(10,20,1)$ uses voltage scales of \SI{10}{\volt} and \SI{20}{\volt} and a current scale of \SI{1}{\ampere}. Comparing states at the same phase separates tracking error from the reference's within-cycle ripple. Under the fixed nominal schedule, convergence of these samples to the anchor also implies convergence between samples to the periodic trajectory, not to a constant physical state.

We use $E_k=0.01$ as a reporting threshold. It is neither a stability boundary nor a hardware accuracy requirement. It is defined relative to the chosen state scales, not to the initial error or the reference components. The condition $E_k<0.01$ implies component errors below \SI{0.10}{\volt}, \SI{0.20}{\volt}, and \SI{0.01}{\ampere} at cycle starts. The joint Euclidean-norm condition is stricter than satisfying these component bounds separately.

We measured the 95th-percentile execution time, denoted p95, for computing the conditioning factor $\beta$. After warm-up, we timed \num{\BetaTimingIndividualSamples} calls using the saved simulation actions as inputs.

The measurements ran on a desktop computer with an \BetaTimingCpu{}, using MATLAB R\BetaTimingMatlabRelease{} and a double-precision C++ implementation compiled with GCC \BetaTimingGccVersion{} and \texttt{-O3} optimization.

In C++, the individual-call p95 reached the timer baseline of \SI{\BetaTimingCppTimerFloorUs}{\micro\second}, so individual execution times could not be resolved reliably. The p95 of the batch-average cost was \SI{\BetaTimingCppBetaBatchPninetyfiveNs}{\nano\second} per call for $\beta$ alone and \SI{\BetaTimingCppFullBatchPninetyfiveNs}{\nano\second} for full conditioning. These batch statistics are not individual-call latency percentiles.

All reported timings were measured on a desktop-class computer, not an embedded system. They exclude acquisition and waveform generation and do not establish execution times or deadline guarantees for embedded hardware.

\section{Discussion}

The results separate dwell feasibility from convergence. For any finite raw action, the dwell inequalities give the largest feasible scale directly. The Lyapunov inequalities test whether the associated linearized dynamics converge as that scale changes. Their common-$P$ certificate is sufficient for uniform exponential stability, but it is not a global nonlinear guarantee.

Neither calculation adds an online optimization problem. The SDP and the supporting invariant-region checks run offline. At each cycle start, the controller only multiplies the error by a fixed gain, computes adjacent differences, and selects the smallest upper bound on the factor.

The method builds on established results. The one-cycle sensitivity follows Patino et al. \cite{patino2010}. Its common-state-matrix reduction recovers Marcolino et al.'s fixed-period timing model \cite{marcolino2021}. Direction-preserving scaling also appears in control allocation \cite{nakamuraZimmerer2026directionPreserving}. Here, the contribution is to obtain that scale in closed form for coupled dwell inequalities and connect the resulting timing feedback to a common-$P$ certificate. We do not propose a new LQR or a new general Lyapunov theorem.

The certificate has two main restrictions. It uses one quadratic function over the whole interval, and it includes the zero-feedback endpoint. It can therefore fail even when a more restricted controller is stable. In this benchmark, open loop already converges to the reference. Feedback improves convergence speed while preserving feasible schedules.

The exact nonlinear simulations demonstrate performance for the chosen initial condition. They do not certify a nonlinear region of attraction.

Hardware effects are also outside the study. The assumed \SI{\AppliedScheduleDwellBound}{\micro\second} bound has not been calibrated experimentally. We do not model timer quantization, delays, dead time, or implementation margin. State estimation, parameter uncertainty, and experimental validation remain to be addressed. The mode sequence, period, and reference phase are fixed throughout.

\section{Conclusion}

The proposed conditioner finds the largest dwell-feasible scalar multiple of a proportional timing action with an $O(N)$ scan. It preserves the action's direction, mode order, and cycle period without an online solver.

An offline endpoint SDP supplies a common quadratic Lyapunov function. This function certifies uniform exponential stability of the conditioned linearized model for every factor sequence in $[0,1]$.

In the converter simulations, conditioning makes the aggressive requests feasible and gives faster convergence than the conservative law and the already stable open-loop schedule. The long-horizon comparison shows the much slower open-loop approach to the same periodic motion. A nonlinear convergence certificate for the full controlled transient requires further analysis.

\section{Feasible-region expansion under timing-action scaling}
\label{app:invariant-region}

\subsection{Expansion of the dwell-feasible region}

A smaller fixed factor allows larger errors while keeping the timing action feasible. To see this, hold $\beta\in(0,1]$ fixed and apply $\dtau=-\beta K\vect{e}$. Substitution into the dwell inequalities gives the feasible region
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal F_\beta&=\{\vect{e}:\beta DK\vect{e}\le\vect{m}\}
    =\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal C,\\
    \mathcal F_1&=\mathcal C.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fixed-beta-feasible}
\end{equation}
At $\beta=1$, this is the Raw-Action Admissible Region $\mathcal C$. Reducing $\beta$ expands the region about the origin by a factor of $1/\beta$ in every direction. Thus $0<\beta_a<\beta_b\le1$ implies $\mathcal F_{\beta_b}\subseteq\mathcal F_{\beta_a}$.

For the three-state benchmark, the volume grows by $\beta^{-3}$. The comparison factors $\LowerComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, $\MiddleComparisonConditioningFactor{}$, and $1$ give volume ratios $\LowerComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, $\MiddleComparisonRegionVolumeRatio{}$, and $1$, respectively. This geometric expansion follows directly from the dwell inequalities. It describes feasibility in the current cycle, not stability over successive cycles.

\subsection{Feasibility over successive cycles}

An error may produce a feasible action now without remaining in the same feasible region at the next cycle. To check feasibility over successive cycles, we keep each comparison factor $\beta$ fixed. The corresponding linearized physical-error dynamics are
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=M_\beta\vect{e}_k,\qquad
    M_\beta=\Phi_x-\beta\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
The region is positively invariant if this map sends every point in it back into the region. For each of the three plotted factors, we checked
\begin{equation}
    M_\beta\mathcal F_\beta\subseteq\mathcal F_\beta.
    \label{eq:fixed-beta-invariance}
\end{equation}
The calculation runs offline in MPT3 \cite{herceg2013mpt3}, using the preimage-containment approach of \cite{gilbert1991maximal}. All three regions pass for this benchmark. Equivalently, every vertex $\vect{v}$ satisfies $\beta DK M_\beta\vect{v}\le\vect{m}$ within numerical tolerance. The benchmark regions are bounded polyhedra, so linearity and convexity extend the vertex checks to all their points. Repeated application of the same fixed-factor controller therefore keeps its linearized trajectory dwell-feasible.

Invariance gives continued feasibility. Convergence follows separately from \cref{thm:common-p}, because $M_\beta=A(\beta)$ in physical error coordinates. Together, these results show that each plotted region admits feasible, convergent linearized operation under its corresponding fixed factor. At $\beta=1$, this is the raw controller on $\mathcal C$. Smaller factors admit larger regions for their respective controllers.

These checks do not prove the same invariance for the exact nonlinear plant. They also use fixed factors, whereas the simulated controller chooses $\beta_k$ from the current error. The plotted expansion is therefore not an enlarged nonlinear region of attraction. The displayed large-error trajectory remains simulation evidence. The common-$P$ theorem covers the conditioned linearized family without relying on these polyhedral checks.

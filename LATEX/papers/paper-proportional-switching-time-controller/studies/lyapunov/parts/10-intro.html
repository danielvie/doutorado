Computing Lyapunov functions by convex optimization

A Lyapunov function is usually taught as something you guess. For a large and useful class of problems you do not have to: fix the shape of V as a quadratic form and the search for it becomes a convex feasibility problem that a solver settles in milliseconds. This study builds that machinery from scratch on a system where every natural guess provably fails, then walks through the certificate used in the switching-time paper, including the part that makes the conditioned signal safe.

What you need

Discrete-time linear algebra, eigenvalues, positive definite matrices. No prior exposure to semidefinite programming. MATLAB with YALMIP and a conic solver (SeDuMi ships with the MPT bundle already on this machine’s path); alternatives that need no extra install are given in §4.

What is here

Six interactive laboratories and a set of diagrams. Everything numeric on this page was produced by studies/lyapunov/export_study_data.m; the two runnable scripts are generic_example.m and paper_certificate.m in the same folder.

1 · Motivation

Why a stability certificate, and why it can be computed

Run a switched system from a hundred initial conditions and watch every trajectory come home. You have learned something about those hundred trajectories. You have learned nothing about the hundred-and-first, and nothing at all about a switching signal you did not happen to try. When the switching is decided at runtime, by a supervisor or by a constraint, the set of futures is uncountable, and simulation can only ever sample it.

The Lyapunov argument replaces the sampling with a bookkeeping trick. Invent a scalar quantity V that is positive everywhere except at the target, and show that every admissible step strictly decreases it. Then no trajectory needs to be simulated: a quantity that is bounded below and drops by a fixed factor at every step has nowhere to go but down.

The difficulty has always been the invention step. Physics sometimes hands you a candidate: stored energy in a circuit, kinetic plus potential energy in a mechanism. When it does, take it. But the moment your state is an abstract error coordinate, say the deviation of a converter’s cycle-start state from a target limit cycle, there is no energy to reach for, and the usual fallback, V(x) = ‖x‖², is very often simply false.

Restricting V to a quadratic form

Restrict V to quadratic forms, V(x) = xₜPx. The unknown is now a symmetric matrix P. Both requirements, positive definiteness of P and decrease of V along every mode, are linear matrix inequalities in P. The set of matrices satisfying them is convex, so “does a Lyapunov function of this form exist?” stops being a matter of ingenuity and becomes a semidefinite program that either returns one or proves there is none.

That is the whole content of this study. Sections 2 to 4 build the machinery on a two-mode system chosen so that every candidate you would reach for by hand is provably wrong. Section 5 applies it to the paper, where the same argument does something that looks surprising the first time: it certifies a closed loop whose gain is being rescaled by an amount nobody knows in advance.

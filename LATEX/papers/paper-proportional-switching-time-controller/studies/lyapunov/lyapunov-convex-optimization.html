Studies · Lyapunov · Convex optimization

Computing Lyapunov functions by convex optimization

A Lyapunov function is usually taught as something you guess. For a large and useful class of problems you do not have to: fix the shape of V as a quadratic form and the search for it becomes a convex feasibility problem that a solver settles in milliseconds. This study builds that machinery from scratch on a system where every natural guess provably fails, then walks through the certificate used in the switching-time paper, including the part that makes the conditioned signal safe.

What you need

Discrete-time linear algebra, eigenvalues, positive definite matrices. No prior exposure to semidefinite programming. MATLAB with YALMIP and a conic solver (SeDuMi ships with the MPT bundle already on this machine’s path); alternatives that need no extra install are given in §4.

What is here

Six interactive laboratories and a set of diagrams. Everything numeric on this page was produced by studies/lyapunov/export_study_data.m; the two runnable scripts are generic_example.m and paper_certificate.m in the same folder.

1 · Motivation

Why a stability certificate, and why it can be computed

Run a switched system from a hundred initial conditions and watch every trajectory come home. You have learned something about those hundred trajectories. You have learned nothing about the hundred-and-first, and nothing at all about a switching signal you did not happen to try. When the switching is decided at runtime, by a supervisor or by a constraint, the set of futures is uncountable, and simulation can only ever sample it.

The Lyapunov argument replaces the sampling with a bookkeeping trick. Invent a scalar quantity V that is positive everywhere except at the target, and show that every admissible step strictly decreases it. Then no trajectory needs to be simulated: a quantity that is bounded below and drops by a fixed factor at every step has nowhere to go but down.

The difficulty has always been the invention step. Physics sometimes hands you a candidate: stored energy in a circuit, kinetic plus potential energy in a mechanism. When it does, take it. But the moment your state is an abstract error coordinate, say the deviation of a converter’s cycle-start state from a target limit cycle, there is no energy to reach for, and the usual fallback, V(x) = ‖x‖², is very often simply false.

Restricting V to a quadratic form

Restrict V to quadratic forms, V(x) = xₜPx. The unknown is now a symmetric matrix P. Both requirements, positive definiteness of P and decrease of V along every mode, are linear matrix inequalities in P. The set of matrices satisfying them is convex, so “does a Lyapunov function of this form exist?” stops being a matter of ingenuity and becomes a semidefinite program that either returns one or proves there is none.

That is the whole content of this study. Sections 2 to 4 build the machinery on a two-mode system chosen so that every candidate you would reach for by hand is provably wrong. Section 5 applies it to the paper, where the same argument does something that looks surprising the first time: it certifies a closed loop whose gain is being rescaled by an amount nobody knows in advance.

2 · Lyapunov theory for discrete-time systems

From the direct method to a linear matrix inequality

2.1  The direct method in discrete time

Everything in this study is discrete time, because the object the paper stabilizes is a cycle-to-cycle map. Take

xk+1 = f(xk),   f(0) = 0.

Suppose a continuous function V satisfies V(0) = 0, V(x) > 0 for x ≠ 0, and

V(f(x)) − V(x) < 0  for all x ≠ 0.

Then the origin is asymptotically stable. If in addition V(f(x)) ≤ q²V(x) for some q < 1, convergence is exponential at rate q. Nothing in the statement mentions trajectories: you check an inequality pointwise, and the trajectories follow.

2.2  Quadratic Lyapunov functions, and the role of P

Choose V(x) = xₜPx with P symmetric and positive definite. Positive definiteness is exactly the “positive except at the origin” requirement, and the level sets {x : xₜPx = c} are ellipsoids. So P fixes what counts as distance: it decides which directions in the state space are treated as far from the origin. This study calls it the ruler for short. P = I is the Euclidean ruler, the one that treats every direction alike. It is one candidate among infinitely many, and there is no reason for a given system to respect it.

For a linear map xk+1 = Axk the decrease condition becomes

V(Ax) − V(x) = xₜ(AₜPA − P)x < 0  for all x ≠ 0,  i.e.  AₜPA − P ≺ 0,

where ≺ 0 means “negative definite”. Two matrix inequalities, then: P ≻ 0 and AₜPA − P ≺ 0.

2.3  Why the two conditions form a convex feasibility problem

Look at what the unknown does. In AₜPA − P, the matrix P appears linearly. There are no products of unknowns and no inverses. A constraint of the form “this symmetric matrix, which depends affinely on the decision variables, is negative definite” is a linear matrix inequality, and the set of decision variables satisfying it is convex. Intersecting several of them, one per mode, keeps it convex.

That is the entire reason this works. A convex feasible set has no local minima to get stuck in and no isolated islands to miss. An interior-point solver either walks into it or returns a certificate that it is empty, and “empty” is a real answer: it means no quadratic Lyapunov function exists for that family, not that you failed to find one.

2.4  Scale normalisation and strict inequalities

Scale is free. If P works, so does 10P: both inequalities are homogeneous. So a solver asked to “maximise the decrease” will happily blow P up without end. Pin the scale down, usually with trace(P) = n, and the problem becomes bounded. This changes nothing about which systems are certifiable; it only removes a ray of identical answers.

Strict inequalities need a margin. Solvers work to a tolerance, so “≺ 0” is asked for as “⩽ −tI” with t a variable to be maximised. A returned t > 0 is a quantitative margin you can re-verify yourself. That last part matters more than it sounds; see §4.4.

2.5  The P-norm and the contraction factor

Once you have P, the useful number is how fast V falls. Define the P-norm ‖x‖P = (xₜPx)1/2 and the induced matrix norm

‖A‖P = maxx ≠ 0 ‖Ax‖P / ‖x‖P = σmax(RAR−1),  where P = RₜR.

‖A‖P < 1 is the same statement as AₜPA − P ≺ 0, and it says every step shrinks the P-norm by at least that factor. Keep two things in mind. First, ‖A‖P ≥ ρ(A) always: no ruler can promise faster than the spectral radius. Second, ‖A‖P is a bound on a single step, which is exactly what makes it survive switching, and ρ(A) is an asymptotic rate for a fixed A, which is exactly why it does not.

2.6  A common P for a family of matrices

Now the step that this whole study is about. Suppose the map applied at step k is not fixed but drawn from a set {A1, …, Am} by a signal you do not control. Ask for the same P to work for all of them:

P ≻ 0,  AiₜPAi − P ≺ 0   for i = 1, …, m.

Still one convex feasibility problem, just with more constraints. And what it buys is out of all proportion to the extra effort: if such a P exists then V falls at every step regardless of which mode fires, so the switched system is exponentially stable under arbitrary switching. That covers every sequence, including ones chosen adversarially against you, and ones that depend on the state. Such a P is called a common quadratic Lyapunov function.

Convex combinations are covered automatically

A common P for the vertices automatically covers every convex combination of them. Since ‖·‖P is a norm, for A(β) = (1−β)A1 + βA2 with β ∈ [0,1],

‖A(β)‖P ≤ (1−β)‖A1‖P + β‖A2‖P ≤ max(q0, q1) < 1.

Two LMIs, and you have covered a continuum. Because the bound is per-step, it is a continuum you may traverse in any order, with any β sequence. That single line is the paper’s Proposition 2.

2.7  The continuous-time counterpart

For ẋ = Ax the same derivation on V̇ = xₜ(AₜP + PA)x gives

P ≻ 0,  AₜP + PA ≺ 0.

Same structure, same convexity, same MATLAB code with one line changed. For a single stable A you do not even need a solver: P = lyap(A', Q) solves AₜP + PA = −Q in closed form, and dlyap(A', Q) does the discrete version. The solver earns its place the moment there is more than one matrix to satisfy at once, which is the next section.

3 · Worked example: a two-mode switched system

A switched system with no obvious Lyapunov function

Here is the system for the rest of this section. State in ℝ², one step per instant, and at every instant an unknown signal picks one of two linear maps:

xk+1 = Aσ(k) xk,    σ(k) ∈ {1, 2},
A1 = [   1.3   0.6 ;  -1  -0.3 ],     A2 = [  -0.1   0.8 ;  -0.3  -1.1 ].

Nothing about it is exotic. Both modes are stable on their own: the eigenvalues of A1 are 0.3 and 0.7, those of A2 are -0.7 and -0.5, so ρ(A1) = ρ(A2) = 0.70. Left running by itself, either mode decays.

And that fact proves nothing whatsoever about the switched system. Section 3.5 shows a pair with identical spectral radii whose alternation diverges. Eigenvalues describe where a fixed map ends up after many steps; a switching signal never gives any map that many steps.

There is also no physics to fall back on. These are abstract coordinates, an error rather than a position, so there is no stored energy to write down. That is the normal situation once you leave textbook mechanics, and it is exactly the situation in §5, where the state is a converter’s deviation from its target limit cycle.

3.1  Testing four candidate Lyapunov functions

Take the four candidates anyone would reach for, in order.

The Euclidean one. V(x) = xₜx decreases under A exactly when ‖A‖2 < 1. Here ‖A1‖2 = 1.7680 and ‖A2‖2 = 1.3730. Both modes stretch some direction, so the Euclidean norm can grow, and under the right switching it grows repeatedly. This is the single most common mistake in the area: a matrix can be Schur and still have norm well above one, because eigenvalues and singular values are different questions.

Mode 1’s own certificate. Solve A1ₜPA1 − P = −I with dlyap. That P certifies mode 1 by construction. Test it on mode 2 and the largest eigenvalue of A2ₜPA2 − P comes back at +1.1557. It fails.

Mode 2’s own certificate. Same construction, tested on mode 1: +3.5841. Fails harder.

A diagonal ruler. Reweighting the coordinates independently, which is the “just scale the states” instinct, means restricting P to be diagonal. Run the same feasibility program over diagonal P only and the solver reports infeasible. No amount of per-axis scaling certifies this pair; the level sets have to be tilted, which needs a nonzero off-diagonal entry.

3.2  The feasible set of certificates, drawn

Because the state is two-dimensional and scale is free, the whole search space fits on a page. Normalise trace(P) = 2 and write

P = [  1 + a   b ;    b   1 − a ],

so every candidate P is one point (a, b) in a plane. P ≻ 0 is the open unit disc a² + b² < 1. Each mode’s decrease condition carves out a further convex region. The lab below scans that plane and shades what survives.

3.3  Solving the semidefinite program

The program is four lines of YALMIP. Maximise the decrease margin t subject to positive definiteness, the trace normalisation, and one decrease constraint per mode:

% studies/lyapunov/generic_example.m
P = sdpvar(2, 2, 'symmetric');
t = sdpvar(1);
C = [P >= 1e-8*eye(2), trace(P) == 2, t >= 0, ...
     A1'*P*A1 - P <= -t*eye(2), ...
     A2'*P*A2 - P <= -t*eye(2)];
optimize(C, -t, sdpsettings('solver', 'sedumi'));

The solver returns

P = [  0.957869  0.804386 ;  0.804386  1.042131 ],    t = 0.043789,

with eigenvalues 0.1945 and 1.8055, so cond(P) = 9.28. The level sets are strongly tilted. The off-diagonal entry is nearly as large as the diagonal ones, which is why no diagonal P could have done the job. The contraction factors are

‖A1‖P = 0.914931,    ‖A2‖P = 0.895469,    q = 0.914931.

Read that as a guarantee with no fine print: whatever the switching signal does, forever, V drops by at least a factor q² = 0.8371 every single step.

The guarantee is easier to believe once you have tried to break it. In the bench below, the adversary policy picks, at every step, whichever mode makes ‖x‖2 grow the most. It is a signal built to defeat the Euclidean ruler.

3.5  A pair for which no common quadratic Lyapunov function exists

The method has a real boundary, and it is worth seeing it. Take

B1 = [  0.75  1.6 ;    0  0.75 ],     B2 = [   0.75    0 ;  -1.6  0.75 ].

Both are Schur, with ρ(B1) = ρ(B2) = 0.75. But ρ(B1B2) = 1.1629 > 1, so the plain alternating signal 1, 2, 1, 2, … diverges geometrically. Switch either lab above to this pair: the trajectory runs off the panel, and the feasible region in Laboratory 1 is empty. The SDP does not struggle or time out. It returns infeasible, which is a theorem about the system, not a complaint about the solver.

When that happens the quadratic class is too small and you enlarge it: a different V per mode with jump conditions, a minimum dwell time so no mode gets cut short, a polytopic or piecewise-quadratic V, or sum-of-squares for genuinely nonlinear dynamics. All of them cost more, and all of them are the right answer only after the cheap test has failed. Run the cheap test first.

4 · Solving the LMI in MATLAB

Three modelling layers, and how to verify the result

The LMI is the same in every case; only the modelling layer differs. All three routes below were run on the pair from §3.

4.1  YALMIP with a conic solver

The one this repository uses, in scripts/generate_results.m and in studies/lyapunov/generic_example.m. You write the inequalities the way you would write them on paper, and YALMIP compiles them for whichever solver is on the path.

function [P, decrease] = common_lyapunov(A)
n = size(A{1}, 1);
P_var = sdpvar(n, n, 'symmetric');
t     = sdpvar(1);

constraints = [P_var >= 1e-8*eye(n), trace(P_var) == n, t >= 0];
for i = 1:numel(A)
    constraints = [constraints, A{i}'*P_var*A{i} - P_var <= -t*eye(n)];
end

diagnostics = optimize(constraints, -t, ...
    sdpsettings('solver', 'sedumi', 'verbose', 0, 'sedumi.eps', 1e-9));
if diagnostics.problem ~= 0
    error('common_lyapunov:infeasible', '%s', diagnostics.info);
end

P = value(P_var);
P = (P + P')/2;                     % kill the asymmetry the solver leaves behind
decrease = value(t);

Three implementation details that matter

trace(P) == n bounds the problem. Without it, maximising t is unbounded, and solvers respond to that by returning nonsense or by failing in a way that looks like infeasibility.

P = (P + P')/2 after value(). The returned matrix is symmetric only to solver tolerance, and an unsymmetrised P makes every later eigenvalue test slightly wrong.

Check diagnostics.problem and fail loudly. Code that keeps going after an infeasible solve will quietly compute a “certificate” from garbage.

4.2  The Robust Control Toolbox LMI Lab

If YALMIP is not available, the Robust Control Toolbox ships an LMI parser. It is older and much more verbose. Each term of each inequality is declared separately, and the sign convention takes a moment to internalise. But it needs nothing beyond MATLAB.

setlmis([]);
P = lmivar(1, [2 1]);              % one full symmetric 2x2 block
lmiterm([-1 1 1 P], 1, 1);         % LMI 1:  P > I
lmiterm([ 1 1 1 0], 1);
lmiterm([ 2 1 1 P], A1', A1);       % LMI 2:  A1'*P*A1 - P < 0
lmiterm([-2 1 1 P], 1, 1);
lmiterm([ 3 1 1 P], A2', A2);       % LMI 3:  A2'*P*A2 - P < 0
lmiterm([-3 1 1 P], 1, 1);
problem = getlmis;
[tmin, xfeas] = feasp(problem);      % feasible when tmin < 0
Pv = dec2mat(problem, xfeas, P);

4.3  CVX

Closest to the mathematical notation of the three. Requires CVX to be installed; it is not on this machine, so unlike the two above this snippet is written from the API rather than from a run.

cvx_begin sdp quiet
    variable P(n, n) symmetric
    variable t
    maximize(t)
    subject to
        P - 1e-8*eye(n) >= 0;
        trace(P) == n;
        for i = 1:numel(A)
            P - A{i}'*P*A{i} - t*eye(n) >= 0;
        end
cvx_end

4.4  Verifying the certificate independently of the solver

A conic solver returns an approximate solution to a tolerance. Near the boundary of the feasible set, which is where the interesting cases sit and where the paper’s own certificate sits, that tolerance can be the difference between a proof and a wrong claim. The certificate is cheap to re-check independently, so re-check it. Four lines:

P = (P + P')/2;                                  % symmetrise first
assert(min(eig(P)) > 0)                          % P really is positive definite
for i = 1:numel(A)
    S = P - A{i}'*P*A{i};  S = (S + S')/2;
    assert(min(eig(S)) > 0)                      % strict decrease, with a margin
    q(i) = sqrt(max(real(eig(A{i}'*P*A{i}, P))));   % the contraction factor
end

The generalised eigenvalue call is worth a note. ‖A‖P2 is the largest λ with AₜPA v = λPv, which eig(A'*P*A, P) computes directly, with no Cholesky factor to form and no explicit inverse. Take real of the result: the pencil is symmetric so the eigenvalues are real, but rounding can leave imaginary parts of order 10−16 that will otherwise contaminate a max.

Two numerical failure modes

A margin that is only numerically positive. If min(eig(P - A'PA)) comes back at 10−11, you do not have a proof, you have a solver artefact. The certificate in §5 has margin 9.7×10−5 with a contraction factor of 0.999939. That is close to the boundary, but comfortably above the noise floor, and the paper says so rather than rounding it to 1.

A badly conditioned P. cond(P) is how eccentric the ellipsoid is. The certificate in §5 has cond(P) = 478, which is fine for a proof but means ‖e‖P and ‖e‖2 can differ by a factor of 22. Never quote one while thinking about the other.

5 · Stability proof of the switching-time controller

Certifying a closed loop whose gain is rescaled at runtime

Everything so far has been preparation for one specific difficulty. The paper’s controller computes a correction, then discovers the correction is physically inadmissible, then shrinks it by a factor decided at runtime from the state. The loop gain is therefore not a constant. It is not even a known sequence. It is a state-dependent scalar that changes every cycle.

The usual reflexes both fail here. You cannot check eigenvalues, because there is no fixed matrix to take eigenvalues of. You cannot simulate your way to a guarantee, because the sequence of gains is uncountable. What is left is a Lyapunov certificate, and it works for the reason given in §2.6, applied to a family of exactly two vertices.

5.1  The object being stabilized: the cycle-start error

The plant is a switched-affine converter that runs a fixed sequence of nine mode intervals, every cycle, forever:

ẋ(t) = Aσx(t) + bσ,    σ drawn from a prescribed nine-interval sequence of period T.

Under the nominal schedule the state traces a periodic trajectory. Sample it once per cycle, at the cycle boundary, and that periodic trajectory collapses to a single point, the cycle anchor x⃗, which is a fixed point of the one-cycle map. Stabilising the limit cycle means driving the cycle-start error ek = xk − x⃗ to zero.

5.2  The linearized one-cycle model

Propagating each interval with an exact augmented matrix exponential and expanding to first order in the interval durations gives a plain linear map from one cycle boundary to the next:

ek+1 = Φek + Γτδτk + higher-order terms,

where δτk ∈ ℝ⁸ holds the eight switching-instant offsets. In the normalised coordinates the paper designs in,

Φ = [  0.999528 -0.003750  0.011229 ;
       0.000878  0.999919  0.003637 ;
       0.005560 -0.000277  0.751737 ]

with ρ(Φ) = 0.999850. The open-loop cycle map is stable, but barely: an error decays by 0.015 % per cycle, and takes 22009 cycles to fall below the paper’s settling threshold. That number matters later, because it is what sets the certificate’s contraction bound.

5.3  The dwell constraint and the conditioning factor β

A static law δτrawk = −Kek, here an LQR gain, knows nothing about time. The physical constraint is on the nine dwell durations, and moving one boundary changes the two dwells on either side of it:

δd = Dδτ,   δdi = δτi − δτi−1,    d̄ + Dδτ ≥ dmin1.

Because the columns of D sum to zero, any offset vector preserves the period exactly. What it does not preserve is admissibility. From the paper’s large-error initial state, the aggressive gain requests offsets up to 65.217 µs and a dwell of -43.045 µs. It is negative, so two boundaries have swapped places and one mode interval has been turned inside out. That schedule cannot be executed.

Clipping the offending components one at a time would fix the dwells and change the direction of the correction, which is the thing the gain was designed to get right. The paper does the opposite: it keeps the direction and gives up magnitude, scaling the entire vector by the largest single factor that restores admissibility,

βk = min{ 1,  mini : qi < 0 mi / (−qi) },    qk = Dδτrawk,   m = d̄ − dmin1,

and applies δτappk = βk δτrawk. One pass over nine numbers; no solver.

5.4  The conditioned closed loop is a line segment

Substitute the applied action into the linearized model:

ek+1 = Φek − ΓτβkKek = [(1 − βk)Φ + βkAcl]ek,    Acl = Φ − ΓτK.

The scalar came out. Whatever the conditioner decides, the closed-loop matrix for that cycle is a point on the line segment joining two fixed matrices: Φ at β = 0, where the correction is switched off entirely, and Acl at β = 1, where it is applied in full. A continuum of possible gains, but a one-dimensional continuum with two endpoints, and §2.6 says two endpoints is all a common P needs.

That is a consequence of scaling by one number. Had the conditioner clipped components independently, the applied action would be Λδτraw with Λ diagonal, and the reachable set of closed-loop matrices would be a polytope with 2⁸ = 256 vertices instead of a segment with two. §5.7 shows what that would have cost.

5.5  Proposition 2: contraction of the whole family

Proposition 2 (common-Lyapunov contraction)

Let P = Pₜ ≻ 0 and suppose ‖Φ‖P ≤ q0 < 1 and ‖Acl‖P ≤ q1 < 1. Then every A(β) with β ∈ [0,1] contracts in the same norm by at most q = max(q0, q1) < 1, so the conditioned linearized model is exponentially stable for every sequence {βk} ⊂ [0,1], including the state-dependent sequences the conditioner actually generates.

Proof. By the triangle inequality for the induced norm, ‖A(β)‖P ≤ (1−β)q0 + βq1 ≤ q < 1. Hence ‖ek‖P ≤ qk‖e0‖P for any sequence. ∎

Notice what the proof does not need. It never assumes β is constant, slowly varying, known ahead of time, or independent of the state. It never needs Φ and Acl to commute. It does not need a dwell-time condition on how long the loop stays at any particular β. All of that is bought by the bound being a statement about one step in a norm that does not depend on which matrix was applied.

And notice what it does need: a common P for both endpoints. In particular ‖Φ‖P < 1, which is a real hypothesis. The uncontrolled cycle map has to be contracting in the same norm. If the open loop were unstable there would be no such P, the conditioner would still run and would still return an admissible schedule, and only the guarantee would be lost. The paper states this restriction rather than burying it.

The bound is set by the open-loop endpoint, not the closed-loop one, and it is 0.999939, a contraction but only just. This is worth being blunt about, because it is easy to over-read a Lyapunov result. The certificate covers the worst conceivable conditioning sequence, and the worst conceivable sequence is βk ≡ 0: a controller that never corrects anything. Nothing can be promised beyond what the open loop does on its own, because the open loop is a member of the family. The bound is a guarantee of stability, not a prediction of performance.

The observed behaviour is a different question with a much better answer. Once βk returns to 1, after 3 cycles, the loop contracts at ρ(Acl) = 0.568151 per cycle, and the run settles at cycle 6 against cycle 20 for the conservative law that never needed conditioning at all.

5.7  Comparison with per-component clipping

The claim in §5.4, that uniform scaling is what makes the family certifiable, is checkable, so here it is checked. Replace the scalar β by a diagonal Λ ∈ [0,1]⁸, one factor per offset. The closed loop becomes Φ − ΓτΛK, and by multilinearity in the diagonal entries the reachable set is the polytope spanned by the 256 vertices with every λi ∈ {0, 1}. 5.8  The local nonlinear statement, and its limits

Everything above is a statement about A(β), which is a linearization. The paper is explicit that the exact nonlinear guarantee is weaker, and local. The argument runs: δτraw(0) = 0, and the nominal schedule keeps a strict dwell margin, so by continuity there is a neighbourhood of the anchor in which the raw action is already admissible and the conditioner never fires. Inside it β ≡ 1, the Jacobian of the exact closed-loop map is Acl, and Acl is Schur. So the anchor, and the phase-locked periodic trajectory through it, are locally exponentially stable for the exact map.

Outside that neighbourhood Proposition 2 still covers arbitrary conditioning, but only for the linearized model. It does not bound the nonlinear remainder, and it is not a global result. The hundred-cycle converter run from a large initial error is evidence, not proof, and the paper labels it that way.

The run is still worth seeing against the envelope, because the gap between them is the point.

6 · Scope of the result, and reproduction

What is proved, what is not, and how to rerun it

A Lyapunov certificate is a narrow, exact statement, and most of the trouble it causes comes from reading it wider than it is. For the result in §5, the line falls here.

ProvedNot proved
Every A(β) on the segment contracts in ‖·‖P by at most q = 0.999939, for arbitrary and state-dependent β sequences. Anything about the nonlinear remainder of the cycle map away from the anchor. The segment result is a statement about the linearization.
The conditioner returns the largest feasible β, preserves the cycle period exactly, and preserves the direction of the raw action. That per-component clipping would also have worked. It would not; see §5.7.
Local exponential stability of the anchor for the exact map, in the neighbourhood where conditioning is inactive and the Jacobian is Acl. A region of attraction. The neighbourhood is shown to exist; it is not estimated.
Stability of the family in the linearized model even when the open loop is the only thing acting. Performance. q is set by the open-loop endpoint and describes the case where the controller never corrects.
Nothing about hardware. Timer quantisation, transport delay, dead time, state estimation, parameter uncertainty, execution time. None of these are in the model.

The habit worth building is to state the family the certificate covers before quoting the number. “q = 0.999939 over the segment between Φ and Acl, for any β sequence in [0,1]” is a claim someone can check. “The system is stable” is not.

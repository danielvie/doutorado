Proving stability with convex optimization

A tutorial: from "does this system settle down?" to a machine-checkable certificate produced by a semidefinite solver. Two worked examples with MATLAB + CVX, two interactive labs, no prior Lyapunov knowledge assumed.

What you will be able to do at the end. Given a dynamical system, you will know how to (1) pose "is it stable?" as a convex feasibility problem, (2) have CVX either hand you a proof object or tell you that no proof of that shape exists, and (3) verify the proof yourself without trusting the solver. Example 1 is a single linear system where everything can also be done by hand, so you can see exactly what the optimizer replaces. Example 2 is a switched system where the hand approach dies and the convex machinery is the only comfortable way through.

1. The question: what does "stable" mean?

Take a system whose state x(t) evolves by a differential equation

x˙=f(x) , x(t)∈ℝn , f(0)=0.

The origin is an equilibrium: start exactly there and you stay there. The stability question is about starting near there. We will use the strongest everyday notion, asymptotic stability: trajectories that start near the origin stay near it and converge to it. For the linear systems in this tutorial the property is global.

For a linear system x˙=Ax you may know the shortcut: stable iff every eigenvalue of A has negative real part. So why bother with anything else? Three reasons.

  • The eigenvalue test gives a yes/no answer but no reusable object. A Lyapunov function is a proof you can hand to someone, differentiate, bound decay rates with, and plug into bigger arguments.
  • The eigenvalue test does not survive contact with anything harder: uncertain dynamics, nonlinear terms, or switching. Example 2 has two matrices whose eigenvalues are both −1 ± i, as stable as you like, and switching between them still almost destroys stability. Eigenvalues of the individual modes say nothing there.
  • The Lyapunov conditions happen to be convex in the unknowns, which turns "find a proof" into a problem a solver can do. That is the whole subject of this tutorial.

2. Lyapunov's idea: one scalar function instead of all trajectories

Lyapunov's move (1892) is to stop chasing trajectories and instead look for a single scalar function V(x), an artificial "energy". Ask two things of it:

(L1) V(x) > 0 for all x ≠ 0, and V(0) = 0.  (a bowl with its minimum at the origin)

(L2) Along every trajectory, V strictly decreases: V˙(x)= ∇V(x)⋅f(x) <0 for all x ≠ 0.

If both hold, every trajectory is a ball rolling down a bowl that has nowhere to rest except the bottom. The state must converge to the origin. Notice what (L2) buys you: you never solve the differential equation. V̇ is computed by the chain rule directly from f, so both conditions are algebraic statements about functions, checkable pointwise.

The catch. Lyapunov's theorem tells you what a proof looks like, not how to find one. For 130 years the textbook answer was "guess something physical and pray". The modern answer: make the search for V a convex optimization problem.

3. From idea to algebra: the quadratic ansatz

To search for a function you need to parameterize it. The classic template is quadratic:

V(x)= xTPx , P=PT ∈ℝn×n.

The unknown is the symmetric matrix P. Its level sets are ellipsoids centered at the origin. Condition (L1) becomes "P is positive definite", written P ≻ 0, meaning xTP x > 0 for every x ≠ 0, equivalently all eigenvalues of P are positive.

For (L2), differentiate along x˙=Ax using the product rule:

V˙ = x˙TPx + xTPx˙ = xT (ATP+PA) x.

So V̇ is again a quadratic form, with matrix ATP + P A. Demanding V̇ < 0 for all x ≠ 0 is exactly negative definiteness of that matrix. The whole stability proof compresses into:

find P such that P≻0  and  ATP+PA≺0.

Any such P is a complete, self-contained proof of global asymptotic stability. Checking a candidate takes two eigenvalue computations. Finding one is the optimization problem.

4. Why this is convex optimization

Look at the shape of the two conditions as constraints on the unknown P:

  • Both are of the form "some matrix, whose entries depend linearly on the unknowns, must be positive (or negative) semidefinite". Such a constraint is called a linear matrix inequality, LMI.
  • The set of matrices satisfying an LMI is convex: if P1 and P2 both satisfy it, so does every blend θP1 + (1−θ)P2. (Positive semidefiniteness survives averaging because quadratic forms do.)
  • An intersection of convex sets is convex. So the set of all valid Lyapunov certificates is one convex blob in the space of symmetric matrices.

Optimizing over such sets is semidefinite programming (SDP), and it sits inside the part of optimization that is essentially solved technology: interior-point methods find a feasible point, or a certificate that none exists, in polynomial time. No initial guesses, no local minima, no tuning. CVX is a MATLAB layer that lets you type the LMIs almost exactly as written above and hands them to an SDP solver.

4.1 One practical wrinkle: solvers hate strict inequalities

"P ≻ 0" is an open condition; numerical solvers work with closed ones (⪯, ⪰). The standard fix exploits scale invariance: if P is a certificate, so is 1000P. So we may normalize and ask for

P⪰I  and  ATP+PA⪯−I,

where M ⪰ I means M − I is positive semidefinite (all eigenvalues of M at least 1). Any strictly feasible P can be scaled to satisfy these, and any solution of these is strictly feasible. Nothing is lost, and the solver gets honest closed constraints with a built-in margin. You will see this trick in every script below.

5. The recipe, step by step

This is the checklist the rest of the tutorial instantiates twice. It looks long written out, but steps 4 through 7 are a dozen lines of CVX.

  1. Normalize the model. Write the dynamics as x˙=f(x) with the equilibrium of interest shifted to the origin.
  2. Choose a template for V whose unknown parameters enter linearly: V = xTP x with unknown P, or a polynomial V = ∑ ck ⋅ (monomial)k with unknown coefficients c. Linearity in the unknowns is what keeps everything convex; the state x is allowed to appear as nonlinearly as it wants.
  3. Write the two Lyapunov conditions (L1) V positive definite and (L2) V̇ negative definite, computing V̇ = ∇V ⋅ f by the chain rule. Because the parameters enter V linearly, they also enter V̇ linearly. If several dynamics must share one V (switched modes, uncertainty vertices), write one copy of (L2) per dynamics.
  4. Convert to matrix inequalities. Quadratic case: the conditions already are matrix definiteness conditions. Polynomial case: write each polynomial as zTQ z for a vector of monomials z and require Q ⪰ 0 (the sum-of-squares trick, Section 7.3).
  5. Strictify. Replace open conditions by closed ones with margins: P ⪰ I,  ATP+PA ⪯ −I, or for polynomials V̇ ≤ −ε‖x‖2m. Scale invariance (Section 4.1) says this costs nothing.
  6. Hand it to the solver. In CVX: declare the unknowns with variable, state each LMI as a line of MATLAB, let it run. Feasibility is the point; the objective is optional.
  7. Read the status honestly. Solved: extract the certificate. Infeasible: no certificate of this template shape exists. That is a mathematical fact, not a solver failure, and the reaction is to enrich the template (higher degree, piecewise structure), not to rerun and hope.
  8. Verify independently. Never let the solver grade its own homework. Check the returned certificate with plain eigenvalue computations or dense grid sampling, using none of the optimization machinery.
  9. (Optional) Optimize over the certificates. Once the feasible set is convex you can pick the best proof in it: largest decay rate, best condition number, largest certified region. Usually one extra constraint plus a bisection.

6. Example 1 (simple): one linear system, two routes to the same proof

The system, chosen small enough to do everything by hand:

x˙=Ax , A= [01 −2−3] , eig(A)= {−1,−2}.

This is y¨+3y˙+2y=0 in first-order form. Eigenvalues in the left half-plane, so we already know it is stable. Perfect: we can concentrate on the process of proving it, and compare the classical hand computation against the convex-optimization computation side by side.

6.1 Route A, by hand: the Lyapunov equation

The classical recipe sidesteps inequalities. Instead of asking ATP + P A ≺ 0, you pick the right-hand side yourself, say −I, and solve the linear equation

ATP+PA=−I.

Write P with unknowns P=[pqqr] and grind out the left side:

ATP+PA = [ −4q p−3q−2r p−3q−2r 2q−6r ] = [ −10 0−1 ]

Three scalar equations, three unknowns, all linear. Solve top-left first, then bottom-right, then off-diagonal:

−4q=−1 ⇒q=14 ; 2q−6r=−1 ⇒r=14 ; p=3q+2r =54.
Phand= [ 1.250.25 0.250.25 ] , eig={0.191,1.309}≻0.

Both eigenvalues positive, so Phand ≻ 0, and by construction V̇ = −‖x‖² < 0. Proof complete. This route works because of a classical theorem: when A is stable, the Lyapunov equation with any chosen negative-definite right side has a unique solution, and it is positive definite. MATLAB's lyap(A', eye(2)) returns exactly the matrix above.

Why this route does not scale. Every degree of freedom got consumed. We had to commit to a right-hand side before solving, there was exactly one solution, and no room remained for extra demands. Ask the same P to also handle a second matrix (Example 2), respect a structural constraint, or maximize a decay rate, and the equation approach has nothing to offer: you would be solving one equation per wish with no guarantee any solution satisfies the rest. Inequalities keep the whole feasible set alive; equations collapse it to a point.

6.2 Route B, by CVX: the Lyapunov inequality

Now the convex-optimization route, following the recipe: template V = xTP x (step 2), the two LMIs with margins (steps 3 to 5), CVX (step 6). The core of matlab/ex1_simple_quadratic.m:

A = [0 1; -2 -3];

cvx_begin sdp
    variable P(2,2) symmetric
    minimize( trace(P) )          % optional; feasibility is the point
    P >= eye(2);                  % L1, normalized
    A'*P + P*A <= -eye(2);        % L2, normalized
cvx_end

Note how literal the transcription is: each mathematical LMI is one line. In sdp mode, >= between matrices means "⪰", the semidefinite order. Actual output on this machine (MATLAB R2025b, CVX with its bundled solver):

cvx_status = Solved
P = 1.8000    0.4000
    0.4000    1.2000
eig(P)          = [1 2]
eig(A'P + PA)   = [-7 -1]     (all <= -1  ->  strict decrease)

A different matrix than Route A, and that is the point: the feasible set is a whole convex region (Figure 3) and the two routes land on different points of it. Both are complete proofs. Verification (step 8) needs no optimization: eig(P) = {1, 2}, positive; eig(ATP+PA) = {−7, −1}, negative. Done.

For a single well-behaved linear system, Route A is honestly the quicker tool, and it is worth seeing that. The optimization route earns its keep the moment you ask for anything more. Here is the smallest such ask.

6.4 Pushing further: certifying a decay rate

Strengthen (L2) to V̇ ≤ −2αV, which by Grönwall's inequality gives V(t) ≤ e−2αtV(0) and hence an explicit envelope on the state norm:

ATP+PA ⪯−2αP ⇒ ‖x(t)‖ ≤ λmax(P) λmin(P) e−αt ‖x(0)‖.

For a fixed α this is still an LMI in P, so "largest certifiable α" is a bisection over α with one small SDP per step. The script does 40 steps and finds:

certified alpha = 1.000002    (theory: min |Re eig(A)| = 1)
norm bound: ||x(t)|| <= 23.9827 * exp(-1.0000 t) * ||x(0)||

The certified rate matches the slowest eigenvalue, which is the best possible, so the quadratic template is tight here. (The 0.000002 overshoot is solver tolerance.) One honest caveat: pushed to the extreme rate, the certificate's P becomes nearly singular and the constant in front, 23.98, gets ugly. Backing off to α = 0.9 gives a far better conditioned P. Optimal certificates tend to live on the boundary of the feasible region, and boundaries are where numerics get uncomfortable. Knowing when to back off 10% is part of the craft.

7. Example 2 (complex): a switched system where quadratic V cannot work

Now the payoff example, chosen to be close to switching-controller territory. Two modes, and the system may jump between them at any moments:

x˙= Aσ(t)x , σ(t)∈{1,2} , A1= [−1−1 1−1] , A2= [−1−10 0.1−1].

Both matrices have eigenvalues −1 ± i. Each mode on its own is a well-damped stable spiral. Intuition says switching between two stable systems should be stable. Intuition is wrong in general: switching can pump energy the way a child pumps a swing, pushing outward with mode 1 where mode 1 moves outward fastest, then handing over to mode 2 where it moves outward fastest. Mode 1 traces circles; mode 2 traces ellipses squashed 10:1. They disagree badly about which directions are "downhill".

To prove stability under arbitrary switching, one function V must decrease along both vector fields at once, everywhere. In the recipe this just means two copies of (L2):

P≻0 , A1TP+PA1≺0 , A2TP+PA2≺0.

Still convex (intersection of three convex sets), still one CVX call. This is the moment Route A physically cannot follow: there is no "Lyapunov equation" for two matrices at once.

7.1 The search fails, and the failure is a theorem

Running the common-quadratic search (matlab/ex2_switched_degree_escalation.m, Part B):

--- Part B: common quadratic V via CVX (expect Infeasible) ---
cvx_status = Infeasible

Because the problem is convex, Infeasible is not "the solver gave up". The solver found a dual certificate proving the constraint set is empty. Read it as a theorem: no quadratic function whatsoever decreases along both modes. Every ellipse you could ever draw is crossed outward by one of the two vector fields somewhere. This is knowledge you could search for by hand for a month and never establish.

For 2×2 pairs there is an independent classical check, worth knowing because it confirms the solver from the outside (Shorten & Narendra): a common quadratic V exists iff neither A1A2 nor A1A2−1 has a real negative eigenvalue. Here (script, Part A):

eig(A1*A2)      = [-0.5283 -7.572]     <-- real and negative: no common quadratic V
eig(A1*inv(A2)) = [0.1701 5.88]

Writing A2 with a parameter a in place of 10 (so mode 2 is squashed a:1), a two-line computation gives trace(A1A2) = 2 − a − 1/a with determinant 4, so the quadratic template dies exactly when a + 1/a ≥ 6, i.e. a ≥ 3 + 2√2 ≈ 5.83. Our a = 10 is well past the wall.

7.2 Is it even stable? A worst-case sanity check first

Before hunting for a fancier certificate, make sure one can exist. Both modes rotate counterclockwise everywhere (their angular speed never vanishes), so the most damaging switching law is easy to characterize: at every angle, use whichever mode grows the radius fastest. Integrating that worst-case radial gain around one revolution (script, Part E):

r(2*pi)/r(0) under worst-case switching = 0.761598   (< 1: contracts, barely)

Two readings. First, the system is stable under arbitrary switching, with about 24% decay per turn even against an adversary. Second, compare each mode alone: a stable spiral with eigenvalues −1 ± i contracts by e−2π ≈ 0.002 per turn. The adversarial switching turned 0.002 into 0.76, a factor-400 loss of contraction. Stability survives by a squeak, and that squeak is exactly why cheap certificates are about to fail.

7.3 Enriching the template: polynomial V and the sum-of-squares trick

The recipe says: infeasible ⇒ enrich the template (step 7). The natural next family after quadratics is homogeneous polynomials of higher even degree. Degree 2m in two states:

V(x)= ∑k=02m ck+1 x12m−k x2k , 2m+1 unknown coefficients.

Two things must generalize from the quadratic case: how to impose "V > 0" and "V̇ < 0" on a polynomial, and both have clean answers.

Differentiation stays linear. By the chain rule, V̇ = ∇V ⋅ Aix is again a homogeneous polynomial of the same degree, and its coefficient vector is a fixed matrix times c: coefficients of V̇|mode i = M(Ai) c. The script builds M(A) in a ten-line function (mder). Linear in the unknowns, so still LMI-compatible.

Positivity becomes a Gram-matrix condition. Checking polynomial nonnegativity is hard in general, but there is a famous convex sufficient condition: write the polynomial as a quadratic form in the vector of degree-m monomials. For m = 2, with z = [x1², x1x2, x2²]T:

V(x)= zTQz , Q⪰0 ⇒ V is a sum of squares  ⇒ V≥0.

The map from Q to coefficients is linear (each coefficient of V is a sum of entries of Q along an anti-diagonal), so "there exists Q ⪰ 0 representing V" is one more LMI, with Q as an extra unknown. Same device for −V̇, with a margin ε(x1²+x2²)m subtracted to force strict decrease. This is the sum-of-squares (SOS) method, and it is nothing more than the quadratic story applied to the lifted vector z instead of x.

A lucky break in two dimensions. In general, SOS is only sufficient for nonnegativity, so an infeasible SOS program leaves a sliver of doubt. But for polynomials in two variables, Hilbert proved (1888) that nonnegative and sum-of-squares coincide. For our planar system the method is lossless: Infeasible at degree 2m means no homogeneous degree-2m Lyapunov function exists, full stop (up to the tiny ε margin). The failures below are certificates of absence, not weakness of the relaxation.

7.4 Degree escalation: turning the crank

Here is the pleasant part. Moving from degree 2 to degree 8 changes nothing in the recipe; the script's search function is generic in m and about 25 lines. The core:

cvx_begin sdp
    variable c(2*m+1)                 % coefficients of V
    variable Q(m+1,m+1)  symmetric    % Gram matrix of V
    variable G1(m+1,m+1) symmetric    % Gram matrix of -Vdot|mode 1 - margin
    variable G2(m+1,m+1) symmetric    % Gram matrix of -Vdot|mode 2 - margin
    Q >= eye(m+1);  G1 >= 0;  G2 >= 0;
    d1 = -mder(A1,m)*c - eps*w;       % w = coeffs of (x1^2+x2^2)^m
    d2 = -mder(A2,m)*c - eps*w;
    for s = 0:2*m                     % match coefficients, anti-diagonal by anti-diagonal
        sum(antidiag(Q, s)) == c(s+1);
        sum(antidiag(G1,s)) == d1(s+1);
        sum(antidiag(G2,s)) == d2(s+1);
    end
cvx_end

7.5 Verify without trusting the solver

Step 8 of the recipe. V is homogeneous, so checking positivity and decrease on the unit circle checks them everywhere (scale any x onto the circle; both quantities scale by r8). The script samples 20001 angles and evaluates V and both V̇'s with plain arithmetic, no CVX anywhere:

--- Part D: grid check on unit circle (20001 points) ---
min V      = 0.982778    (> 0)
max Vdot|1 = -0.100517   (< 0)
max Vdot|2 = -0.020969   (< 0)
certified decay: Vdot <= -2*alpha*V with alpha = 0.004966

Positive bowl, strictly decreasing along both modes. The certified worst-case decay rate α ≈ 0.005 is small, and it should be: Section 7.2 showed the true worst-case contraction is weak. A tight certificate for a nearly-marginal system is honest, not timid.

Since the whole pipeline is automatic, mapping how difficulty grows with the squash parameter a costs one loop (script, Part F). Minimal certificate degree found:

The story in one row: quadratics hold out exactly until the theoretical wall 3 + 2√2 ≈ 5.83 from Section 7.1, quartics carry the load through a = 9, our a = 10 needs degree 8, and by a = 11 even degree 10 fails (the worst-case spiral of Section 7.2 is by then expanding or nearly so; stability itself is on the edge or gone). Certificate degree is a measurable ruler for "how close to unstable is this system", and you get it by turning a crank.

8. What to take away

  • A Lyapunov function is a proof object. Two sign conditions, checkable by eigenvalues or grid evaluation, and stability follows for all initial conditions with no simulation.
  • Finding one is convex whenever the unknown parameters enter V linearly. Quadratic templates give LMIs directly; polynomial templates give LMIs after the SOS/Gram lift. Solvers return either a certificate or a proof that the template is too weak, and both answers are trustworthy.
  • Equations vs. inequalities is the real difference between the classical and convex routes. Example 1: the equation route is fine for one system but consumes all freedom; the inequality route keeps a convex set of proofs alive, which is why extra demands (a second mode, a decay rate) are one added line each.
  • Infeasibility is information. Example 2's ladder (degree 2, 4, 6 fail; 8 works) is a quantitative measurement of how fragile the switched system is, made of nothing but failed searches.
  • Always verify independently. Eigenvalues for quadratic certificates, dense sampling for homogeneous polynomial ones. It costs seconds and converts "the solver said so" into "I checked".

Where this goes next

Everything here extends along the same convex spine. Multiple/piecewise Lyapunov functions with dwell-time constraints handle switched systems where no common V of any degree exists (the a = 12 column). The S-procedure brings in state-region conditions ("V̇ < 0 only where mode i is active"), which is the natural language for switching controllers rather than arbitrary switching. Robust LMIs certify whole polytopes of uncertain A's at once (that is what the Robust Control Toolbox automates with lyap-style solvers behind musyn/hinfsyn machinery). And SOSTOOLS or YALMIP's sos module automate the Gram bookkeeping we did by hand, for any number of states, where Hilbert's two-variable luck no longer holds and SOS becomes a (still remarkably strong) sufficient condition.

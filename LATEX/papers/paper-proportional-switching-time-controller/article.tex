\title[Dwell-conditioned switching-time feedback]{Dwell-Conditioned Cycle-to-Cycle Switching-Time Feedback for Mode-Dependent Switched-Affine Systems}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Daniel} \sur{Vieira}}\email{danielvie@gmail.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Roberto Kawakami Harrop} \sur{Galv\~ao}}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Systems and Control}, \orgname{Instituto Tecnol\'ogico de Aeron\'autica}, \orgaddress{\city{S\~ao Jos\'e dos Campos}, \state{S\~ao Paulo}, \country{Brazil}}}

\abstract{A static cycle-to-cycle feedback law can hold a switched-affine plant on a periodic trajectory by displacing its switching instants, but the offsets it requests may shorten a mode interval below the minimum dwell duration, or even reverse it. We repair the requested action instead of redesigning the law. The complete vector of raw switching-instant offsets is multiplied by the largest factor in $[0,1]$ that keeps every dwell duration admissible. Because one scalar multiplies the whole vector, the requested direction, the mode order, and the cycle period all survive, and a single scan of the $N$ dwell changes returns the factor in $O(N)$ time without an online solver. In the linearized cycle model the conditioned closed loop is $A(\beta)=(1-\beta)\Phi+\beta A_{\mathrm{cl}}$, a segment between the nominal and the raw closed-loop matrix, so one quadratic Lyapunov function that contracts both endpoints certifies every sequence of conditioning factors. We obtain the cycle model by exact augmented matrix-exponential propagation over a prescribed sequence of affine modes, letting the state matrix and the affine term change together. The derivation recovers the known switching sensitivity and reduces to the switched-actuator model as a special case. On a nine-interval DC--DC converter benchmark, the analytical Jacobians match central differences to \PhiJacobianRelativeError{} and \GammaJacobianRelativeError{}. From a large initial error, an aggressive raw law requests a \MinimumRawDwell{}~\si{\micro\second} dwell. Conditioning restores the assumed \AppliedScheduleDwellBound{}~\si{\micro\second} bound and still settles faster than a conservative law that never violates it. The Lyapunov result covers the linearized family, and the nonlinear guarantee is local.}

\keywords{switched-affine systems, periodic trajectories, switching-time control, dwell-time conditioning, one-cycle map, DC--DC converter}

\section{Introduction}

Power converters set voltage and current by switching semiconductor devices in a repeating pattern. Their controllers work under hard limits: a fixed switching frequency, a minimum duration for every switch configuration, current and voltage bounds, and a short interval in which to decide. Predictive control enforces such limits explicitly, at the cost of an optimization problem solved online, and much recent work attacks that cost. Saeed et al. shrink the decision space with a Laguerre parameterization \cite{saeed2022laguerre}, Yang et al. build a solver specialized to fixed-frequency operation \cite{yang2022fixed}, and others prune or approximate the candidate set \cite{augustine2023mpc,switchedOptimalDwell2025}. Static feedback removes the online optimization altogether. It does not remove the limits, and the action it requests can still be impossible to apply.

This paper works in one narrow setting. A prescribed sequence of affine modes repeats with a fixed period, and once per cycle the controller displaces only the interior switching instants. The mode order, the number of intervals, and the two cycle boundaries stay fixed, so the target is a periodic trajectory rather than an equilibrium. The actuator is therefore different from state-dependent mode selection, where the controller decides which mode is active \cite{egidio2022rank,russo2026dwell}, and from online schedule optimization, where a solver rebuilds the cycle at every step. Here one cycle-start error produces one static correction to the switching instants of the next cycle.

Two published results supply the cycle model. Pati\~no, Riedinger, and Ruiz derived switching-instant sensitivities for cyclic systems whose dynamics depend on the active mode and used them inside a predictive controller \cite{patino2010}. Marcolino, Galv\~ao, and Kienitz fixed the period and derived a one-cycle model, a discrete LQR timing law, and a dwell-constrained predictive controller for an LTI plant driven by switched actuator levels \cite{marcolino2021}. Their predictive controller enforces the dwell limits. Their standalone LQR comparison instead saturates the offsets one instant at a time, which can move the final switching instant, and the reported response diverges \cite{marcolino2021}. That example exposes the general difficulty. Each dwell duration depends on two adjacent offsets, so an unconstrained timing action can violate the limits, and clipping the offsets individually changes the direction the law asked for. Trading online optimization for static feedback does not by itself yield a schedule the converter can execute.

We close that gap without touching the feedback law. Given the raw offset vector, we apply the largest scalar multiple of it that keeps every dwell duration at or above the minimum. Scaling the whole vector by one number preserves its direction, the mode order, and both cycle boundaries, and one pass over the $N$ dwell changes finds that number, so the online cost is $O(N)$ and no solver runs. In the linearized cycle model the conditioned closed loop is $A(\beta)=(1-\beta)\Phi+\beta A_{\mathrm{cl}}$, so a single quadratic Lyapunov function that contracts $\Phi$ and $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ certifies every conditioning sequence the plant can generate. These two results are the contribution. Everything else supports them: the fixed-period one-cycle model for mode-dependent affine dynamics, which recovers the published sensitivity structure, Jacobians checked against finite differences, and an exact nonlinear simulation of a nine-interval converter benchmark. The LQR laws are comparison controllers, not a new synthesis method. The nonlinear guarantee is local, and the converter study is simulation rather than hardware validation. \Cref{sec:model} derives the cycle model, \cref{sec:conditioning} builds the conditioner and its certificate, and \cref{sec:case-study} reports the converter study.

\section{Fixed-period one-cycle model}
\label{sec:model}

The conditioner of \cref{sec:conditioning} needs two things from the plant: how a switching-instant offset changes the next cycle-start error, and what dwell durations that offset produces. This section derives both, in four steps. We fix the timing coordinates, propagate one cycle exactly in an augmented state, expand that product to first order in the dwell changes, and rewrite the result in the independent switching-instant offsets.

\subsection{Switched-affine cycle and timing coordinates}

Consider
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\vect{x}}(t)=A_{\sigma(t)}\vect{x}(t)+\vect{b}_{\sigma(t)},
    \qquad \vect{x}(t)\in\R^n,
    \label{eq:plant}
\end{equation}
where each cycle follows the fixed mode sequence $\bm{\Omega}=(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_N)$ with nominal boundaries
\begin{equation}
    0=\bar t_0<\bar t_1<\cdots<\bar t_N=T,
    \qquad \bar d_i=\bar t_i-\bar t_{i-1}.
\end{equation}
The nominal schedule generates a reference periodic trajectory $\bar{\vect{x}}(t)$. Its cycle anchor $\vect{x}^{\star}=\bar{\vect{x}}(0)=\bar{\vect{x}}(T)$ is a fixed point of the nominal cycle, and we measure the cycle-start error against it as $\vect{e}_k=\vect{x}_k-\vect{x}^{\star}$.

Only the interior switching instants move:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    t_{k,i}&=\bar t_i+\delta\tau_{k,i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\\
    \delta\tau_{k,0}&=\delta\tau_{k,N}=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The vector $\dtau_k$ contains the $N-1$ independent offsets. The corresponding $N$ dwell changes satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \ddwell_k=D\dtau_k,
    \qquad
    D_{ij}=\begin{cases}
        1,&i=j,\\
        -1,&i=j+1,\\
        0,&\text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:incidence-map}
\end{equation}
for $i=1,\ldots,N$ and $j=1,\ldots,N-1$, so $\delta d_{k,i}=\delta\tau_{k,i}-\delta\tau_{k,i-1}$. Moving one instant later lengthens the interval before it and shortens the interval after it by the same amount. Since $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$, the dwell changes sum to zero and every offset vector preserves the cycle period $T$. We write the exact one-cycle map as $\vect{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal P(\vect{x}_k,\dtau_k)$.

\subsection{Exact augmented propagation}

Affine dynamics do not compose as a product of transition matrices, because each interval carries its own forced term. Appending a constant coordinate to the state removes that obstacle. Define the augmented state, the projection back onto the physical state, and the mode generators as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal X=\begin{bmatrix}\vect{x}\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    \Pi=\begin{bmatrix}I_n&\vect{0}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
    F_i=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\omega_i}&\vect{b}_{\omega_i}\\
    \vect{0}^{\mathsf T}&0\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:augmented-generator}
\end{equation}
On interval $i$ the augmented dynamics are linear, $\dot{\mathcal X}=F_i\mathcal X$. Write $E_i(s)=e^{F_i s}$ for a transition of duration $s$, and $\phi_i=E_i(\bar d_i)$ for the nominal one. The applied durations are $d_{k,i}=\bar d_i+\delta d_{k,i}$, so one cycle propagates exactly as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal X_{k+1}=
    E_N(\bar d_N+\delta d_{k,N})\cdots
    E_1(\bar d_1+\delta d_{k,1})\mathcal X_k.
    \label{eq:exact-augmented-cycle}
\end{equation}
Products act from right to left, so the rightmost factor covers the first interval. Nothing has been approximated yet. Let
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathcal X}_i=\phi_i\cdots\phi_1\bar{\mathcal X}_0,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\vect{e}}_k=
    \begin{bmatrix}\vect{e}_k\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Periodicity gives $\phi_N\cdots\phi_1\bar{\mathcal X}_0=\bar{\mathcal X}_0$, so subtracting the nominal cycle from \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-cycle} leaves the exact augmented terminal error
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \widetilde{\vect{e}}_{k+1}={}&
    E_N(\bar d_N+\delta d_{k,N})\cdots
    E_1(\bar d_1+\delta d_{k,1})\\
    &\times(\bar{\mathcal X}_0+\widetilde{\vect{e}}_k)
    -\phi_N\cdots\phi_1\bar{\mathcal X}_0.
\end{split}
\label{eq:exact-augmented-error}
\end{equation}

\subsection{First-order expansion in dwell durations}

Only the interval durations vary in \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-error}, and each one enters through a single matrix exponential. Because $F_i$ is constant on interval $i$, differentiating with respect to that duration gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}e^{F_i s}=F_i e^{F_i s},
\end{equation}
and each perturbed transition satisfies
\begin{equation}
    E_i(\bar d_i+\delta d_{k,i})
    =\phi_i+F_i\phi_i\,\delta d_{k,i}
    +\mathcal O(\delta d_{k,i}^2).
    \label{eq:interval-first-order}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:interval-first-order} into the ordered product and keeping the terms with at most one dwell perturbation, with $E_i=E_i(\bar d_i+\delta d_{k,i})$, gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    E_N\cdots E_1={}&\widetilde\Phi
    +\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \phi_N\cdots\phi_{i+1}F_i\phi_i\cdots\phi_1\,
    \delta d_{k,i}\\
    &+\mathcal O(\|\ddwell_k\|^2),
    \qquad \widetilde\Phi=\phi_N\cdots\phi_1,
\end{split}
\label{eq:ordered-product-first-order}
\end{equation}
where an empty product is the identity. The nominal term cancels when this expansion enters \eqref{eq:exact-augmented-error}. Products of the form $\delta d_{k,i}\widetilde{\vect{e}}_k$ pair an error with a dwell change and are second order, so we drop them. Using $\phi_i\cdots\phi_1\bar{\mathcal X}_0=\bar{\mathcal X}_i$, the first-order terms that remain are
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\vect{e}}_{k+1}
    =\widetilde\Phi\widetilde{\vect{e}}_k
    +\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\vect{g}}_i\delta d_{k,i}
    +\mathcal O\!\left(\left\|[\vect{e}_k^{\mathsf T}\ \ddwell_k^{\mathsf T}]\right\|^2\right),
    \label{eq:augmented-dwell-linearization}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\vect{g}}_i
    =\phi_N\cdots\phi_{i+1}F_i\bar{\mathcal X}_i.
    \label{eq:augmented-dwell-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Projecting onto the physical state gives
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=\Phi\vect{e}_k+G_d\ddwell_k
    +\mathcal O\!\left(\left\|[\vect{e}_k^{\mathsf T}\ \ddwell_k^{\mathsf T}]\right\|^2\right),
    \label{eq:dwell-linearization}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \Phi=e^{A_{\omega_N}\bar d_N}\cdots e^{A_{\omega_1}\bar d_1},
    \qquad
    G_d=\Pi[\widetilde{\vect{g}}_1\ \cdots\ \widetilde{\vect{g}}_N].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Switching-instant offsets}

Substituting $\ddwell_k=D\dtau_k$ into \eqref{eq:dwell-linearization} gives the model in the independent timing coordinates
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}&=\Phi\vect{e}_k+\Gamma_\tau\dtau_k
    +\mathcal O\!\left(\left\|[\vect{e}_k^{\mathsf T}\ \dtau_k^{\mathsf T}]\right\|^2\right),\\
    \Gamma_\tau&=G_dD.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cycle-model}
\end{equation}
Column $i$ of $D$ increases dwell $i$ and decreases dwell $i+1$. Therefore $\Gamma_{\tau,i}=\Pi(\widetilde{\vect{g}}_i-\widetilde{\vect{g}}_{i+1})$. Since $F_{i+1}$ commutes with $\phi_{i+1}=e^{F_{i+1}\bar d_{i+1}}$, factoring the adjacent terms yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}={}&e^{A_{\omega_N}\bar d_N}\cdots
    e^{A_{\omega_{i+1}}\bar d_{i+1}}\\
    &\times\left[(A_{\omega_i}-A_{\omega_{i+1}})\bar{\vect{x}}(\bar t_i)
    +\vect{b}_{\omega_i}-\vect{b}_{\omega_{i+1}}\right].
\end{split}
\label{eq:timing-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The bracket is the jump in the affine vector field at the switching state, from mode $\omega_i$ to mode $\omega_{i+1}$. That is what displacing instant $i$ does. It trades time between two adjacent modes without changing the state at that instant to first order, and the remaining nominal transitions carry the resulting jump to the end of the cycle. Equation~\eqref{eq:timing-jacobian} therefore has the switching-sensitivity structure reported in \cite{patino2010}, obtained here from dwell perturbations in independent fixed-period timing coordinates.

If every interval instead shares $A_{\omega_i}=A_c$ and $\vect{b}_{\omega_i}=B_c\vect{u}_{i-1}$, then
\begin{equation}
    \Phi=e^{A_cT},\qquad
    \Gamma_{\tau,i}=e^{A_c(T-\bar t_i)}B_c(\vect{u}_{i-1}-\vect{u}_i),
    \label{eq:marcolino-specialization}
\end{equation}
which matches Marcolino's switched-actuator timing model, apart from the sign convention for the input jump \cite{marcolino2021}. When $A_{\omega_i}$ depends on the mode, the jump also depends on the switching state, so this reduction does not apply in general.

\section{Solver-free dwell-time conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

\Cref{sec:model} says what an offset does, not whether it can be applied. A static law knows nothing about the minimum dwell duration, and \eqref{eq:incidence-map} ties each dwell to two adjacent offsets, so clipping the offsets one at a time would change the direction of the correction. We keep the direction and give up magnitude instead, then certify the closed loop that results.

Let a static feedback law produce the raw switching-instant offset
\begin{equation}
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}=-K\vect{e}_k,
    \qquad A_{\mathrm{cl}}=\Phi-\Gamma_\tau K.
    \label{eq:raw-law}
\end{equation}
Let $d_{\min}$ be the required minimum dwell duration and assume that the nominal schedule keeps a strict margin above it,
\begin{equation}
    \vect{m}=\bar{\vect{d}}-d_{\min}\vect{1}>\vect{0}.
    \label{eq:strict-margin}
\end{equation}
Each $m_i$ is how much dwell $i$ can be shortened before it becomes inadmissible. The raw action is feasible for exactly those errors in the polyhedron
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal C=\{\vect{e}:\bar{\vect{d}}-DK\vect{e}
    \ge d_{\min}\vect{1}\}.
    \label{eq:admissible-region}
\end{equation}
For $\vect{e}_k\in\mathcal C$ the raw action needs no modification. Outside $\mathcal C$ at least one dwell would fall below $d_{\min}$. Collect the requested dwell changes in $\vect{q}_k=D\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}$ and set
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \beta_k&=\min\left\{1,\ \min_{i:q_{k,i}<0}
    \frac{m_i}{-q_{k,i}}\right\},\\
    \dtau_k^{\mathrm{app}}&=\beta_k\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conditioner}
\end{equation}
where a minimum over an empty set is $+\infty$. We call $\beta_k$ the conditioning factor. It equals one whenever the raw action is already feasible, and otherwise the dwell that runs out of margin first decides it.

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility and maximality]
\label{prop:conditioner}
Under \eqref{eq:strict-margin}, \eqref{eq:conditioner} returns the largest $\beta\in[0,1]$ such that every applied dwell duration satisfies $\bar{\vect{d}}+D(\beta\dtau_k^{\mathrm{raw}})\ge d_{\min}\vect{1}$. The conditioned action preserves the cycle period and the direction of the raw action. Computing it takes $O(N)$ time and requires no online solver.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A dwell with $q_{k,i}\ge0$ cannot be shortened by scaling. A dwell with $q_{k,i}<0$ requires $\beta\le m_i/(-q_{k,i})$. Intersecting these bounds with $[0,1]$ gives exactly \eqref{eq:conditioner}, which is therefore both feasible and maximal. The identity $\vect{1}^{\mathsf T}D=0$ preserves $T$, and multiplying the full action by one nonnegative scalar leaves its direction unchanged. A single pass over the $N$ dwell changes evaluates every bound.
\end{proof}

In the linearized model, the applied action gives
\begin{equation}
    \vect{e}_{k+1}=A(\beta_k)\vect{e}_k,
    \qquad
    A(\beta)=(1-\beta)\Phi+\beta A_{\mathrm{cl}}.
    \label{eq:conditioned-family}
\end{equation}
Conditioning therefore cannot produce an arbitrary saturated gain. It produces a matrix on the segment between the nominal cycle matrix $\Phi$, obtained at $\beta=0$ when the correction is switched off, and the raw closed-loop matrix $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$, obtained at $\beta=1$ when the correction is applied in full.

\begin{proposition}[Common-Lyapunov contraction]
\label{prop:common-lyapunov}
Let $P=P^{\mathsf T}>0$, $\|\vect{x}\|_P=(\vect{x}^{\mathsf T}P\vect{x})^{1/2}$, and suppose
\begin{equation}
    \|\Phi\|_P\le q_0<1,
    \qquad \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|_P\le q_1<1.
    \label{eq:endpoint-norms}
\end{equation}
Then every $A(\beta)$ with $\beta\in[0,1]$ contracts in the same norm by a factor no greater than $q=\max(q_0,q_1)<1$. The conditioned linearized model is therefore exponentially stable for every sequence $\{\beta_k\}\subset[0,1]$, including the state-dependent sequences generated by \eqref{eq:conditioner}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The induced-norm triangle inequality gives
$\|A(\beta)\|_P\le(1-\beta)q_0+\beta q_1\le q<1$.
Therefore $\|\vect{e}_k\|_P\le q^k\|\vect{e}_0\|_P$ for any sequence.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis on $\Phi$ deserves attention. It requires the uncontrolled cycle map to contract in the same norm, so the certificate is available only for plants whose nominal cycle is already stable. Without it the conditioner still runs and still returns an admissible schedule, and only the guarantee is lost. When such a $P$ does exist, it can be computed offline from endpoint Lyapunov inequalities, which are standard semidefinite constraints \cite{boyd1994lmi}. Online execution only evaluates the matrix--vector feedback law, $D\dtau^{\mathrm{raw}}$, and the scalar scan in \eqref{eq:conditioner}.

The nonlinear statement is weaker, and local. Suppose $\mathcal P$ is continuously differentiable while the mode order is preserved, and that $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is Schur. The strict nominal margin and $\dtau^{\mathrm{raw}}(\vect{0})=\vect{0}$ put a neighborhood of the anchor inside $\mathcal C$. Inside that neighborhood the conditioner never acts, $\beta=1$, and the Jacobian of the exact closed-loop map is $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$, so the exact map exponentially stabilizes the cycle anchor and the phase-locked reference periodic trajectory through it. Beyond that neighborhood, \cref{prop:common-lyapunov} still covers arbitrary conditioning, but only for the linearized model. It does not prove global stability of the nonlinear plant.

\section{Converter case study}
\label{sec:case-study}

The case study asks three questions of the theory. Does the derived one-cycle model reproduce the exact plant? Does the certificate of \cref{prop:common-lyapunov} hold for a concrete design? And does conditioning deliver anything that an already-feasible controller cannot?

\subsection{Three-cell converter benchmark and implementation}

We use the three-cell multilevel DC--DC converter studied by Pati\~no et al. \cite{patino2010}, with state $\vect{x}=[v_{C_1},v_{C_2},i_L]^{\mathsf T}$. For binary switch commands $u_1,u_2,u_3\in\{0,1\}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_{\vect{u}}&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&(u_2-u_1)/C_1\\
0&0&(u_3-u_2)/C_2\\
(u_1-u_2)/L&(u_2-u_3)/L&-R/L
\end{bmatrix},\\
\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\Eu_3/L\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:converter}
\end{equation}
Both $A_{\vect{u}}$ and $\vect{b}_{\vect{u}}$ depend on the switch state. The plant therefore does not satisfy the common-state-matrix assumption in \eqref{eq:marcolino-specialization}. \Cref{tab:parameters} collects the converter, schedule, and feedback parameters used in this section.

The reported physical mode sequence is $(0,1,3,7,2,0,4,7,4)$, the rounded cycle boundaries are $(0,66,88,110,132,154,220,242,264,286)$~\si{\micro\second}, and the reported cycle anchor is $\vect{x}^{\star}=(9.9247,19.2928,0.9823)^{\mathsf T}$. Because those values are rounded, exact propagation through them does not return to the anchor. We therefore adjust the dwell vector once by least squares, holding the published \SI{\NominalPeriodMicroseconds}{\micro\second} period, the reported anchor, and the \SI{\BenchmarkDwellBound}{\micro\second} nominal-design bound. The resulting schedule is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \bar{\vect{t}}={}&(0,63.889,87.557,109.557,132.538,154.538,\\
    &218.210,240.380,263.828,286)~\si{\micro\second}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:benchmark-schedule}
\end{equation}
The largest boundary correction is \MaximumBoundaryCorrection{}~\si{\micro\second}, and the closure error in the infinity norm is \NominalClosureError{}. The adjustment prepares the benchmark data. It is not a trajectory-design method.

\subsection{Linearization and certificate checks}

We check the analytical Jacobians against central differences of the exact one-cycle map. The state steps are $10^{-4}$ times the diagonal entries of $S_x$, and the timing step is $10^{-3}t_s=\SI{0.01}{\micro\second}$. A second check applies simultaneous state and timing perturbations with normalized amplitude $\epsilon$ and evaluates
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    r(\epsilon)=\biggl\|S_x^{-1}\bigl[&\mathcal P(\vect{x}^{\star}+\Delta\vect{x},\dtau)
    -\vect{x}^{\star}\\
    &-\Phi\Delta\vect{x}-\Gamma_\tau\dtau\bigr]\biggr\|_2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian errors and residual slope in \cref{tab:results} confirm the implemented first-order model. The relative errors sit at the level of numerical noise, and the residual decays with slope \LinearizationResidualSlope{}, the second-order decay a correct first-order model must show. The generated data include the full analytical and finite-difference matrices and the residual sweep.

For the aggressive normalized closed loop, an offline semidefinite program returns a common $P$. The endpoint norms are $\|\Phi\|_P=\PhiPNorm{}$ and $\|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|_P=\AggressiveAclPNorm{}$, so \cref{prop:common-lyapunov} certifies the whole conditioned linearized family with contraction bound \SegmentContractionBound{}. The open-loop endpoint sets that bound, because it is the slower of the two. The bound is therefore a worst case over arbitrary conditioning sequences, including the sequence that never applies the correction, and it does not describe the observed rate. Once $\beta_k$ returns to one, the closed loop contracts at $\rho(A_{\mathrm{cl}})=\AggressiveSpectralRadius{}$. The controller solves no semidefinite program online.

\section{Discussion}

The conditioner keeps the largest feasible scalar multiple of a static timing action and changes neither its direction nor the cycle boundaries. That is also its limit. It cannot look ahead, and it cannot choose a different direction when the requested one is nearly blocked, so it does not replace constrained optimization for controllers that must do either. The search for a common $P$ runs offline and costs nothing online.

The cycle model joins two earlier formulations. Pati\~no et al. derived the mode-dependent switching sensitivity \cite{patino2010}, and Marcolino et al. formulated fixed-period timing coordinates, dwell inequalities, and LQR for switched actuators \cite{marcolino2021}. Our derivation connects the two and checks the result numerically. The new control result is the scalar conditioner and its common-Lyapunov certificate, not the LQR design.

The \SI{\AppliedScheduleDwellBound}{\micro\second} minimum dwell is a simulation assumption rather than a hardware figure. The study leaves out timer quantization, delays, dead time, implementation margin, state estimation, model uncertainty, and experiments, and it fixes the mode order, phase, and period. The exact nonlinear guarantee holds only in a neighborhood where conditioning never acts, so the large-error simulation is evidence for this benchmark and not a global nonlinear result.

\section{Conclusion}

The conditioner enforces every minimum-dwell constraint in $O(N)$ time and keeps both the direction of the raw action and the cycle period. One quadratic Lyapunov function for the two endpoint matrices certifies the entire conditioned linearized family, including the state-dependent conditioning sequences the plant generates. The augmented derivation behind those matrices covers mode-dependent switched-affine dynamics and reduces to the switched-actuator model when the state matrix is common, and finite differences confirm the Jacobians it produces. On the converter benchmark, conditioning turns an infeasible aggressive action into an admissible one, and the response still settles sooner than that of a conservative law chosen to be feasible throughout. The nonlinear guarantee remains local, and hardware tests are still needed to account for timer quantization, delays, and implementation margin.
